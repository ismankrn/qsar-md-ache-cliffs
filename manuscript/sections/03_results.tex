\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Validation of the docking protocol}\label{sec:dockval}

We validated the protocol by redocking donepezil into 4EY7, which is the structure from
which its crystallographic pose is taken. A docked pose can only be scored against a
crystallographic answer, and no ligand of the dataset has one. Donepezil spans both ends
of the gorge, so the validation covers a pose that engages the catalytic and the
peripheral anionic site at once rather than one site alone. It was docked with five
independent seeds, and a pose was counted as successful below \SI{2.0}{\angstrom} RMSD
from the crystal pose, which is the conventional criterion. We recovered the crystal
pose at $0.745 \pm 0.009$~\AA{} in five of five runs, as Figure~\ref{fig:redock} shows.
This clears the criterion by nearly a factor of three. The spread of
\SI{0.009}{\angstrom} across the five seeds shows the recovery to be a property of the
protocol rather than a fortunate draw.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{Figure2_redocking_validation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Redocking validation.} Donepezil redocked into 4EY7, its own
    structure, against the crystal pose, with Trp86 and Tyr337 marked.}
  \label{fig:redock}
\end{figure}

The pose-geometry and molecular-dynamics descriptors are defined in terms of residue
contacts and not of RMSD. We therefore validated the protocol a second time at the
level of interactions. Interaction fingerprints were computed for the crystal pose and
the redocked pose of donepezil by using ProLIF \citep{prolif2021}, with bond orders
transferred from the docked structure so that the two poses carry identical chemistry
and differ only in their coordinates. The crystal binding mode is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:interactions}(a), and Figure~\ref{fig:interactions}(b) sets the two
fingerprints side by side, with every interaction placed by the residue it involves and
by its type. Counting residues alone understates what changes, because a residue
contacted in both poses can still gain or lose an interaction, as Tyr341 does. Counted
interaction by interaction, the crystal pose carries 18 and the redocked pose 14. Of
these, 11 are common, 7 appear only in the crystal pose and 3 only in the docked one.
Ten of the 18 crystal interactions are typed rather than bare van der Waals contact,
and 8 of those 10 are recovered. Among them are the hydrophobic and $\pi$-stacking
contacts at Trp86 and at Trp286 and the cation--$\pi$ and hydrophobic contacts at
Tyr337, which are the six that fall at the three sites the descriptors use. The two
typed interactions that are lost are a hydrogen-bond acceptor contact with Phe295 and a
hydrophobic contact with Phe297, and neither lies at one of the three named sites.
Every remaining difference is van der Waals contact, lost at Tyr72, Trp286, Ser203,
Ser293 and His447 and gained at Tyr124, Tyr341 and Gly448. Every typed interaction at
the three sites on which the descriptors of this work rest is therefore reproduced, and
the disagreement is confined to the weakest class of contact. We used the typing for
validation only, and it enters no descriptor set.

\subsection{Stability and thermodynamic state of the simulations}\label{sec:simqc}

The production ensembles ran at the thermodynamic state they were set to, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:thermo}. The temperature in panel~(a) averages \SI{300.00}{\kelvin}
with a run-to-run spread of \SI{0.02}{\kelvin}. The density in panel~(b) averages
\SI{1018.9}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed} with a spread of
\SI{0.12}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}. The potential energy in panel~(c) drifts by
\SI{-10.5}{\kilo\joule\per\mole\per\nano\second} at the median, against a total near
$-1.3\times10^{6}$~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole}, which is less than one part in $10^{5}$
per nanosecond. Panel~(d) shows the running mean of the pressure, which ends at
\SI{1.00}{\bar}. We show a running mean because the instantaneous pressure of a box of
this size has a within-run spread of about \SI{89}{\bar} and carries no information
frame by frame. These four quantities were computed on 64 of the 229 development runs. The subset
establishes only that the simulations were well behaved, and no comparison between
descriptor sets rests on it. What the four panels establish is that the integrator, the
thermostat and the barostat behaved. A drift below one part in $10^{5}$ per nanosecond
also rules out a systematic leak of energy.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_thermo.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Production ensembles ran at the intended thermodynamic state.}
    (a)~Temperature, (b)~density, (c)~potential energy and (d)~the running mean of

Structural stability across the campaign is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stability}. The
receptor C$\alpha$ RMSD in panel~(a) has a median of \SI{1.42}{\angstrom} across runs,
and no frame of any run exceeds \SI{2.20}{\angstrom}. The steep rise in that panel is
complete within the five nanoseconds that are discarded, and the curve creeps only
slowly over the window that is analysed. The descriptors are therefore not measured on
a system still finding its equilibrium. The ligand RMSD from its equilibrated pose in
panel~(b) has a median of \SI{2.99}{\angstrom}. We drew the two production designs
separately instead of averaging them into a single band whose composition would change
partway along its own axis. They track each other over the window they share, at
\SI{1.41}{\angstrom} against \SI{1.44}{\angstrom} for the receptor and
\SI{3.06}{\angstrom} against \SI{2.84}{\angstrom} for the ligand. The runs that
continued are therefore not a different population, and the common window may be read
as one set. A ligand displacement of three \AA{} is a rearrangement within the site and
not a departure from it, because the centre of mass of no ligand in any of the 229
development runs ever moved more than \SI{12}{\angstrom} from where it started.

The fluctuation in panel~(c) is concentrated where the receptor was cut. Its eight
largest values belong to residues 494, 541, 493, 258, 540, 4, 492 and 539, reaching
\SI{4.53}{\angstrom} at residue 494, and every one of them is a capped segment terminus
or its immediate neighbour. That is the expected behaviour of a free chain end, and it
is confined to the surface. The fourteen gorge-lining residues fluctuate by a median of
\SI{0.49}{\angstrom} and by no more than \SI{0.75}{\angstrom}. Splitting the chain into
three segments therefore left the binding site as rigid as the rest of the fold, which
is the condition the contact descriptors depend on. All three panels were computed
after the same periodic-boundary treatment that the descriptors receive.

Taken together, the two figures establish that the systems are equilibrated and
structurally stable over the window that is analysed, which is the condition the
descriptors need. They do not establish that the sampling has converged. A temperature
and a density settle within picoseconds of any production run, and an RMSD plateaus
just as convincingly for a ligand that entered one basin early and stayed in it. The
replicate pilot measures that directly, by running the same ligand again under a
different velocity seed. The share of descriptors whose seed-to-seed spread swamps
their spread between ligands does not fall as the window lengthens. It stands at 19 of
32 at \SI{5}{\nano\second}, 21 at \SI{20}{\nano\second} and 20 at
\SI{45}{\nano\second}. A converging simulation would show that share shrinking, and it
does not. What these two figures rule out is therefore a system that is drifting,
unfolding or losing its ligand, which is a weaker statement than convergence and the
one the descriptors actually require.

\subsection{Comparison of the five descriptor sets}\label{sec:ladder}

Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} gives the best score each rung reached on each framing,
together with the algorithm family that reached it. On \framingA{} the 2D fingerprints
lead at $+0.386$. The receptor ensemble is second at $+0.215$, MM/GBSA reaches
$+0.118$, molecular dynamics $+0.068$ and the single rigid pose $+0.046$. On
\framingB{}, which is the endpoint that defines a cliff, the order changes. The
receptor ensemble reaches $+0.274$ against $+0.200$ for the fingerprints, molecular
dynamics reaches $+0.152$, pose geometry $+0.167$, and MM/GBSA falls below zero at
$-0.056$. On \framingC{} every rung separates cliff pairs from controls better than
chance. The fingerprints lead at $0.805$ and molecular dynamics follows closely at
$0.796$, with MM/GBSA at $0.753$, the ensemble at $0.705$ and the single pose at
$0.684$. Every rung was scored on all 217 development ligands and all 145 development
pairs, so no rung is helped by an easier subset of the data. The winning algorithm
family also differs between cells rather than one family carrying a rung throughout,
which Tables~\ref{tab:benchreg} and~\ref{tab:benchclf} show in full.

    arms and the algorithm families, with the winning family in parentheses. The full
    grid is in the supplementary tables.}

No rung is resolvably ahead of any other on any framing of the development split, and
the ordering above should be read with that in mind. The largest advantage any
structure-based rung holds over the fingerprints anywhere is the $0.074$ by which the
receptor ensemble leads on \framingB{}. The largest lead the fingerprints hold over the
best structure-based rung is $0.171$ on \framingA{} and $0.010$ on \framingC{}. All
three are well inside the resolution of about $0.3$ that this design can claim, so none
of them is a difference it can see. The claim is therefore not that two-dimensional
fingerprints win everywhere, because on \framingB{} the ensemble is numerically ahead
of them. The claim is that no rung is separated from any other by more than the noise.
The one comparison that does separate is the gap between rung~4 and the fingerprints on
\framingA{}, and it runs against rung~4.

The headline comparison gives rung~1 thousands of features and rung~4 twelve. The
obvious objection is therefore that the fingerprint wins on width rather than on
content. We scored an arm in which the fingerprint is cut to the width of rung~4 by a
univariate filter fitted inside each training fold. The width is twelve features on
framings~A and~B and twenty-four on \framingC{}, where each pair contributes a
concatenation of two vectors. Table~\ref{tab:matcheddim} gives the result. The control
does not settle the question in one direction. It reverses the ordering on two framings
and leaves it standing on the third. Meanwhile, the two framings whose ordering changes
are the two the design cannot resolve, and the one framing it can resolve is the one
left unchanged.

We read that as a statement about the control rather than as evidence for rung~4,
because the two sides are not handicapped symmetrically. The twelve rung-4 features are
what a reliability criterion left of a set designed for this target. The twelve
fingerprint bits are what a univariate filter left of two thousand that carry their
information in combination rather than singly. Cutting a fingerprint to twelve bits
does not produce a smaller fingerprint. It produces a different and much poorer
descriptor. The arm therefore shows that feature count is not the quantity on which
this comparison turns, which is worth establishing but is not a result about molecular
dynamics. The comparison that matters is therefore against rung~1 as it would actually
be used, with every feature it has and the feature set re-selected inside each
bootstrap replicate.

Figure~\ref{fig:gapforest} shows the gap against that unhandicapped baseline. The
bootstrap over units puts it at $-0.375$ on \framingA{}, with a 95 per cent interval of
$[-0.596, -0.184]$. It puts the gap at $-0.093$ on \framingB{} with $[-0.319, +0.127]$,
and at $+0.003$ on \framingC{} with $[-0.102, +0.103]$. Only \framingA{} is resolved, and
it is resolved against rung~4 rather than for it. That interval is the only one on the
development split that excludes zero. Its sign matters as much as its width, because it
does not report rung~4 as unproven but as worse than a fingerprint on per-ligand
potency. On the two pair framings the gap is small and the interval contains zero in
both, so neither supports a claim in either direction, and the figure colours the three
accordingly. The baseline throughout is rung~1 with every feature it has, its feature
set re-selected inside each replicate, so the gap is not measured against a weakened
fingerprint. On the development split the molecular-dynamics descriptors therefore fail
to improve on a 2D baseline where the design can see, and leave the question open where
it cannot.

\subsection{Statistical power of the comparison}\label{sec:power}

We measured the resolution of the comparison on synthetic descriptor matrices pushed
through the same support vector machine and the same frozen folds, on the pair endpoint
of \framingB{}. The baseline in that simulation is itself synthetic. We built it by
tuning the signal strength of a descriptor block until its out-of-fold coefficient of
determination matched the $0.200$ that rung~1 reaches, which the tuning met at $0.209$.
Figure~\ref{fig:power}(a) gives, against that baseline, the share of simulations in
which a candidate of a given strength is called the better of the two. A candidate
scoring $0.335$ is called better in 36 per cent of them and one scoring $0.479$ in 90
per cent, while 80 per cent power arrives only at about $0.45$. The curve runs along
the bottom until roughly $0.3$ and climbs steeply after it. The design is therefore not
merely less sensitive to a small advantage but close to blind to one. The dash-dotted
line marks where rung~4 landed, which is to the left of the baseline and inside that
flat stretch.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3\_power.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Detection limit of the comparison, and the assay-noise ceiling.}
    (a)~Power to call a candidate descriptor set the better of the two, against a
    synthetic baseline tuned to the $0.200$ of rung~1 and re-simulated inside every
    replicate, with the dash-dotted line marking where rung~4 landed. (b)~Width of
    the 95\% interval on the gap across the same range of candidates. (c)~The ceiling
    that assay reproducibility imposes, from the 336 of 5{,}609 compounds carrying a
    repeat, against where each rung lies.}
  \label{fig:power}
\end{figure}

Panel~(b) carries the same limit as a width rather than as a probability. The 95 per
cent interval on the gap is $0.32$ wide on average and stays between $0.28$ and $0.34$
across every candidate tested, so the limit barely depends on how strong the candidate
is. The portable statement is therefore that this study separates two values of the
coefficient of determination about $0.3$ apart, and not less. That number was fixed
before the comparison it bounds had been scored, so it describes the design rather than
the outcome. It also bounds the null rather than leaving it open, and both halves of
the bound deserve stating. Differences larger than about $0.3$ are excluded, so the
claim that molecular dynamics adds a large advantage over fingerprints is ruled out on
this dataset. Differences smaller than that are not addressed at all, so a modest
advantage remains possible and this study cannot speak to it. What would change the
bound is the number of pairs rather than the length of the trajectories, because the
width follows from 145 pairs.

Panel~(c) closes the one remaining way of reading the result, which is that the
endpoint itself is too noisy to predict. A model cannot explain more of the variance
than the measurement reproduces. We estimated that ceiling from the 336 of 5609
compounds carrying a repeat assay. Each compound's spread across its repeats gives a
noise variance, and the ceiling is the share of the observed variance that a perfect
model could still account for. The panel draws that ceiling for three readings of the
spread, whose median is $0.06$ log units, whose mean is $0.18$ and whose upper decile
is $0.56$, and shades the variance above it as unreachable. Even the pessimistic
reading taken from the upper decile leaves every rung of the ladder far below the
shaded band. The variance the ladder fails to explain is therefore real signal and not
assay noise. This places the limitation in the descriptors rather than in the data, and
removes the reading that there was nothing there to find.

\subsection{The held-out split}\label{sec:testsplit}

Everything to this point was measured on the development split, which also chose the
descriptor arm, the algorithm family and the reliability filter for every rung. A
separate 54 ligands and 38 pairs, which are 20 cliffs against 18 matched controls, were
sealed at the outset and never scored. All five rungs cover them, the dynamic
descriptors included, so the split can test the whole ladder and not the four rungs
that need no trajectory. We opened it once, under rules fixed in advance. The
replicate filter was not recomputed, and no model was retrained or reselected. Each rung kept the arm and the family that
won on development, refitted on all 217 development ligands and scored a single time.

On \framingA{} and \framingC{} nothing changes, because all eight gaps against the
fingerprint baseline on those two framings contain zero, as they do on development. On \framingB{}
the result runs the other way. The receptor ensemble leads the fingerprints by $+0.283$
with a 95 per cent interval of $[+0.107, +0.462]$, and molecular dynamics leads them by
$+0.311$ with an interval of $[+0.114, +0.500]$, and both intervals exclude zero. On
development the same two gaps were $+0.074$ and $-0.048$, neither of them resolvable,
and the second ran against rung~4. Table~\ref{tab:testsplit} gives every rung's score
on the test units beside its gap against the fingerprints, with a 95 per cent interval
from 20{,}000 bootstrap replicates over pairs and the corresponding development gap.
Rung~1 is the baseline the gaps are measured from, so it carries a score and no gap,
and the metric is $R^2$ on framings~A and~B and the area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve on \framingC{}.

  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{The held-out split against the development split.} The $p$ values
    are Holm- and Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted across all twelve gaps, and an asterisk
    marks a gap that survives the Holm correction.}
  \label{tab:testsplit}
  \input{../results/28_test_split_gaps.tex}

We checked three things before reading anything into this. First, the splits share no
ligands, no ligands between the pairs of one split and the pairs of the other, and no
connected components, so the difference is not leakage from the way the pairs were
assembled. Second, twelve gaps were tested at once, which would be expected to produce
about 0.6 false positives at a five per cent threshold. Both surviving gaps clear a
Holm correction and a Benjamini--Hochberg correction across all twelve. The twelve
tests share the same 38 pairs and the two structure-based rungs share pose information,
so Holm is conservative here rather than exact. Third, we dropped each test pair in
turn. This moves the rung-4 gap only within $[+0.280, +0.365]$, and no single pair
brings it to zero, so the gap does not rest on a handful of extreme observations.

What the test split does not do is overturn the development result, and the reason is
visible in the levels rather than the gaps. Every rung scores higher on the test pairs
than on the development folds. The fingerprints move from $+0.200$ to $+0.362$ and
MM/GBSA from $-0.056$ to $+0.506$, which says that these 38 pairs are easier rather
than that the ladder has begun to work. Only the difference between two rungs measured
on the same pairs is insensitive to that shift. That is why we report the difference,
and why it is the difference that was corrected and resampled. Both surviving gaps are
about $0.3$, which is the resolution this design was measured to have before any of it
was scored. Neither result therefore contradicts that bound, because the development split saw
nothing of that size and the test split saw something of exactly that size.

The two splits therefore disagree, on one design and one scoring path, and 38 pairs
against 145 cannot settle which reading is right. A test sample that happens to suit
low-dimensional descriptors would produce this pattern, and so would the difference
between a cross-validated score and a single refit. Neither can be separated from a
real advantage with a held-out set of this size. We report the disagreement rather than
either half of it. What it does change is the confidence with which the development
result can be stated. A negative finding that the held-out data point away from is a
weaker negative finding, and the quantity that would resolve it is the same one that
bounds everything else here, which is the number of pairs.

\subsection{Reliability of the dynamic descriptors}\label{sec:reliability}

Which descriptors a trajectory can carry at all was settled by the replicate pilot.
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}(a) shows the ratio it measures for every descriptor at
every analysis window. Of the 160 combinations of descriptor and window, 60 clear the
threshold of 2 and 100 do not, and the failures are not spread evenly over the three
design classes. Persistence quantities clear it in 53 of 100 combinations, fluctuation
quantities in 3 of 35, and event counts in 4 of 20. Panel~(b) gives this as the
composition of verdicts at each window. The remaining five combinations are the
receptor backbone RMSD, which is carried as a quality-control quantity rather than as a
member of any design class and never clears the threshold. The fraction of the window
over which a contact is held is therefore reproducible across velocity seeds. Meanwhile
the amplitude of motion within that window, at this trajectory length, largely is not.
The threshold itself was fixed before any replicate had been scored, so the division is
not a line drawn around the answer.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4_reliability.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Reliability of each descriptor against the replicate test.}
    (a)~Ratio of the spread across ligands to the spread across replicates of the same
    ligand, for each descriptor at each analysis window, against the threshold of 2
    fixed before the pilot ran. (b)~Verdict composition by window, with the
    descriptors grouped by design class.}
  \label{fig:reliability}
\end{figure}

The event counts behave as a class only in the sense that three of their four members
never survive. Every one of the four combinations that does is the rate of bridging
episodes, which clears the threshold at 5, 10, 20 and \SI{45}{\nano\second}. It fails
only at \SI{30}{\nano\second}, which is the one window where the between-ligand spread
rests on 45 ligands rather than on all 217. That failure therefore reflects the sample
rather than the descriptor. The longest bridging episode is dominated by the seed at
every length. The Tyr337 rotamer count is either too sparse to assess or
seed-dominated, and whether the ligand left the gorge is constant because no ligand
ever did. A rate can therefore survive a replicate test, but only where the events it
counts are frequent enough to be counted reliably in the window available.

At the \SI{20}{\nano\second} window that production uses, 11 of the 32 descriptors
pass, and 9 of those are contact occupancies. That concentration is the practical
finding of this subsection and the one least tied to this target. Anyone building
descriptors from trajectories of this length should expect persistence-type observables
to survive a replicate test and fluctuation-type observables not to, and should measure
that rather than assume it. The filter also earns its place within this study rather
than remaining a diagnostic. The filtered set reaches $+0.152$ on \framingB{} against
$-0.054$ for the unfiltered one, and $0.796$ on \framingC{} against $0.721$. Removing
the descriptors that measure the seed therefore improves what remains on both pair
endpoints, which is what a filter of this kind is supposed to do and is not guaranteed
to achieve.

\subsection{Interpretation of the distance-based contact occupancies}\label{sec:typed}

Two of the eleven descriptors that survive the replicate test are \texttt{occ\_cas} and
\texttt{occ\_pas}. These count the fraction of frames in which a ligand heavy atom lies
within \SI{4.5}{\angstrom} of Trp86 or Trp286. That criterion is geometric and says
nothing about whether a chemical interaction is present. We therefore recomputed typed
interaction fingerprints over the trajectories by using ProLIF \citep{prolif2021} and
compared the two. Figure~\ref{fig:dyninteractions}(a) gives the median occupancy of
each type at each gorge-lining residue. Each cell is a median over the runs in which
that interaction is detected, so it describes how persistent an interaction is when it
forms rather than how often it forms. The typing was computed on 21 frames of each run,
which resolves an occupancy to about five percentage points, so differences smaller
than that should not be read. Hydrophobic engagement at Trp86 and at Trp286 occupies
the whole window at the median, and it is detected in 91 and 92 per cent of runs.
$\pi$-stacking reaches 70 and 57 per cent of the window, in the 76 and 79 per cent of
runs that show it at all. The cation--$\pi$ contact that these cationic ligands were
expected to form is the weakest of the three on both counts. It holds 43 per cent of
the window at Trp86 and 38 at Trp286, and it appears in 58 and 29 per cent of runs.
Eight of the 229 development runs could not be typed because ProLIF reported a
fragmented residue,
so the panel rests on 221.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_dynamic_interactions.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Typed interaction occupancy, and what the distance-only
    descriptors summarise.} (a)~Median occupancy of each interaction type at each
    gorge-lining residue, taken over the runs in which that interaction is detected
    rather than over all 221, with cells left blank where it is detected in fewer than
    a tenth of them. The typing was computed on 21 frames of each run, 46 for the runs
    that were continued, so a cell resolves occupancy to about five percentage points. (b)~The typed occupancy of any type at
    Trp86 and Trp286 against the distance-only \texttt{occ\_cas} and \texttt{occ\_pas}
    of this work, one point per run, against the diagonal on which the two would
    agree.}
  \label{fig:dyninteractions}
\end{figure}

Panel~(b) places the typed occupancy against the distance-only one, run by run. The two
are not computed on the same frames. The distance-only occupancy uses all 2000 frames
of the window while the typing uses 21, so a small disagreement in either direction is
expected and a few points sit above the diagonal. The Spearman coefficients of $0.61$
at Trp86 and $0.55$ at Trp286 overstate the agreement, because both measures sit at 100
per cent for 77 per cent of runs at Trp86 and 56 per cent at Trp286. Most of the
coefficient is therefore ties. Recomputed on the runs where at least one measure is
below saturation, they fall to $0.39$ and $0.22$. The informative statistic is the
signed difference, which has a median of zero but reaches $+75$ and $+77$ percentage
points. There are therefore runs in which the ligand sits within \SI{4.5}{\angstrom} of
the tryptophan for nearly every frame while ProLIF recognises no interaction there at
all.

The disagreement is not evidence that one measure is right and the other wrong, because
neither is a ground truth and the typed criteria are thresholds of their own. The
distance-only descriptors measure what they are defined to measure, which is proximity,
and they measure it reproducibly. What the panel shows is that proximity saturates. For
the ligands that are not simply pinned against the site, which are exactly the ones a
cliff comparison turns on, the descriptor therefore returns the same value whatever the
chemistry is doing. That bears on the replicate test, because a quantity sitting at 100
per cent in most runs is reproducible for a reason that has nothing to do with its
usefulness. A reliability filter will therefore favour a saturated quantity over a
discriminating one, which is a property of that filter worth stating rather than a
fault in these descriptors.

The typed occupancy is not a ready replacement for it either, and the same replicate
test says why. Put through that test, it gives a ratio of $2.63$ at Trp86 and $1.05$ at
Trp286, against $103$ and $52$ for the distance-only descriptors. One of the two would
therefore not clear the threshold of two at all. Part of that noise is the coarser
sampling, and a typing computed on every frame might fare better. The exchange is
therefore discrimination against reproducibility rather than chemistry against
geometry, and this work has tested neither side of it as a predictor. Nothing in this
subsection enters any model, because a typed descriptor family introduced after the
ladder had been scored would be post hoc. We leave it as a question for a later study.

\subsection{Dependence on trajectory length}\label{sec:duration}

We tested whether \SI{20}{\nano\second} is longer than the descriptors need by
recomputing them on the first 5, 10, 20 and \SI{30}{\nano\second} of each trajectory
and scoring each window on the frozen folds. Two series are drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:duration}, because the replicate filter selects a different set at each
window. It passes 13 descriptors at 5 and \SI{10}{\nano\second} against 11 at 20, which
with the heavy-atom covariate make 14 features against 12. Letting it vary would
therefore confound how much trajectory a descriptor saw with which descriptors were
chosen. The fixed series holds the \SI{20}{\nano\second} selection at every window and
is the one that isolates length. On it, \framingA{} in panel~(a) moves between $+0.030$
and $+0.044$, \framingB{} in panel~(b) between $+0.149$ and $+0.196$, and \framingC{}
in panel~(c) between $0.748$ and $0.796$. Each of those spreads is smaller than the
interval the corresponding framing carries, so none of them is a dependence this design
can resolve.

The \SI{30}{\nano\second} point is drawn apart because only the 45 development ligands
whose runs were continued reach it, and on \framingA{} it rests on 11 pairs rather than
145. We show it so that the curve does not appear to stop for want of data. It should
not be read as a fall at longer trajectories, because the sample changes with it. Those
45 are the ligands whose runs happened to be continued rather than a random draw from
the 217, so the two series are not interchangeable even at the windows they share.
Nothing in the three panels supports the view that the simulations were too short,
which is the first objection a null result from molecular dynamics attracts. What they
establish between them is that trajectory length is not the lever. The descriptors are
no better at \SI{20}{\nano\second} than at 10. The practical reading is that they can
be computed from \SI{15}{\nano\second} of simulation rather than the 50 the original
design called for, which answers the cost objection with a measurement rather than an
argument.

One number in these panels should not be quoted on its own. The $+0.196$ that
\framingB{} reaches at \SI{10}{\nano\second} is the best of six cells computed
together, and reporting it as the performance of molecular dynamics at 10 nanoseconds
would be exactly the selection this paper exists to warn against. The spread across the
three windows is the quantity to read, not its maximum. What would move the result is
not the length of the trajectories but the number of pairs, because the resolution of
about $0.3$ follows from 145 of them. More nanoseconds do not change it, and more pairs
or more targets are the only things that would.

\subsection{Scope of the comparison}
\label{sec:synthesis}

We carried five descriptor sets up a ladder of physical detail, from two-dimensional
fingerprints through the geometry of a docked pose, the same geometry over a receptor
ensemble, an implicit-solvent energy and finally a molecular dynamics trajectory. Each
met three framings of the same data on the same frozen splits, which are 217
development ligands and 145 development pairs of one well-characterised target. What
the design can see was fixed before any of it was scored, and it held. The 95 per cent
interval on a gap is $0.32$ wide on average and lies between $0.28$ and $0.34$ whatever
the strength of the candidate. This study therefore separates two coefficients of
determination about $0.3$ apart and not less.

Within that limit the answer on the development split is uniform. The largest advantage
any structure-based rung holds over the fingerprints anywhere is the $0.074$ by which
the receptor ensemble leads on the signed pair difference. That is a quarter of what
the design can resolve, so on the development split no rung of the ladder is resolvably
better than a fingerprint on any of the three framings. The one comparison that does
resolve runs the other way. On per-ligand potency the gap between the trajectory
descriptors and the fingerprints is $-0.375$ with an interval of $[-0.596, -0.184]$,
which is the only interval on the development split that excludes zero. Its sign
carries as much as its width, because it reports the dynamic descriptors not as
unproven but as worse. On the two pair framings the intervals contain zero and support
no claim in either direction. The held-out split of Section~\ref{sec:testsplit} does
not reproduce that uniformity, and we report the disagreement there rather than
resolving it here.

Two readings would make that result uninteresting, and neither survives. The first is
that the endpoint is too noisy to predict, and the 336 of 5{,}609 compounds carrying a
repeat assay answer it. Even the pessimistic reading of their spread, which is the
upper decile at $0.56$ log units, leaves every rung of the ladder far below the
variance that assay reproducibility puts out of reach. The variance the ladder fails to
explain is signal and not noise, which places the limitation in the descriptors rather
than in the data. The second reading is that the simulations were too short. Holding
the descriptor set fixed and varying only the trajectory, per-ligand potency moves
between $+0.030$ and $+0.044$, the signed pair difference between $+0.149$ and $+0.196$
and the classification between $0.748$ and $0.796$. Every one of those spreads is
smaller than the interval its framing carries, so none is a dependence this design can
resolve, and nothing in them suggests a curve still climbing at the point it was cut.

What a trajectory can carry at all was settled separately, and it constrains how the
null should be read. Of 160 combinations of descriptor and analysis window put to a
replicate test, 60 clear the threshold and 100 do not, and the failures fall by design
class rather than at random. Persistence quantities clear in 53 of 100 combinations,
fluctuation quantities in 3 of 35 and event counts in 4 of 20. How long a contact is
held reproduces across velocity seeds, while how far things move, at this trajectory
length, largely does not. The null therefore applies to a trajectory read for
persistence, which is most of what survives a reproducibility criterion, rather than to
every quantity a trajectory could in principle supply.

One measurement artefact quantified along the way generalises past this target and past
molecular dynamics. A descriptor filter re-fitted at each trajectory length selects
fourteen quantities at the shortest window and twelve at the longest, and not the same
ones. A curve built that way therefore confounds how much trajectory a descriptor saw
with which descriptors were chosen. Read that way the ladder appears to rise
monotonically with trajectory length. Held to one selection it is flat. Any study that
varies a parameter and re-selects features at each of its values is exposed to this,
and the remedy costs nothing beyond fixing the selection once and carrying it across.

These results occupy a gap in the existing evidence rather than contradicting it.
\citet{husby2015} reports that structure-based methods predict activity cliffs
accurately. The figure behind that claim comes from template docking onto the crystal
pose of the stronger ligand, together with a pocket ensemble selected by using the
cliff labels, which those authors describe as retrospective. Their prospective arm,
cross-docking, sits at a median of 53 per cent. This study is the prospective case run
with a matched ligand-based baseline and a stated detection limit, and its answer is
consistent with that arm rather than with the abstract. What would move any of this is
not more nanoseconds. The width of the interval follows from the number of pairs, so
more pairs and more targets are the only things that would narrow it.

\subsection{Limitations}

The central limitation is the number of pairs. One target supplies 145 development
pairs, and from them follows a 95 per cent interval about $0.3$ wide on the coefficient
of determination. On the development split this study therefore excludes a large
advantage for the structure-based descriptors and says nothing about a modest one. The
held-out split carries 38 pairs and a wider interval still, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:testsplit} reports a disagreement between the two rather than a
revision of the first. A single target also cannot separate a property of these
descriptors from a property of this binding site, and whether the result holds elsewhere
is not addressed here.

Each ligand carries one production run, so there are no production replicates and no
error bar on an individual ligand's descriptor. What the replicate test measures comes
instead from a pilot of six ligands simulated three times each. Six ligands are a
narrow basis for a criterion then applied to 32 descriptors across four analysis
windows. The threshold was fixed before any replicate had been scored, which protects
the division from being drawn around the answer but not from being imprecise.

The headline comparison is not matched on width. The fingerprints carry 2{,}265
features at their widest against either 12 or 33 for the trajectory descriptors, and we
did not equalise them. The arm that cuts the fingerprint to the width of rung~4
reverses the ordering on the two framings the design cannot resolve and leaves the
third standing, but it is a robustness check rather than a controlled experiment.
Cutting a fingerprint to twelve bits does not yield a narrower fingerprint so much as a
different and much poorer descriptor. The arm therefore bounds the influence of feature
count without isolating it.

The bootstrap resamples units inside the frozen folds. This places duplicates of the
same unit on both sides of a fold boundary and inflates the absolute level of every
score it reports. That inflation applies to both sides of a gap and largely cancels
there, so the intervals on the gaps are usable. The medians, however, are not
performance estimates and should not be quoted as such.

The dynamic contact descriptors are distance-only. Where a ligand sits within the
cutoff of a residue without forming a typed interaction, the occupancy credits an
engagement that a chemical reading would not, and the two disagree by as much as 77
percentage points on individual residues. Those descriptors are reproducible across
velocity seeds, which is what they were filtered for, but reproducible is not
interpretable. The per-residue quantities should therefore not be read as a chemical
account of binding.

Cliff and control pairs differ in molecular size, which could separate the two groups
through a descriptor that measures nothing but size. We tested nineteen pose-geometry
and energy descriptors against that confound. Four separated the groups in a raw test,
and none of those four survived either stratification on size or adjustment for size as
a covariate, so the confound is controlled rather than ignored. The same 145 pairs
bound that test as well, and it would not detect a small residual effect.
