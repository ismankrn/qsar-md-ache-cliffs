\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Validation of the docking protocol}\label{sec:dockval}

We validated the protocol by redocking donepezil into 4EY7, the structure from which
its crystallographic pose is taken, since a docked pose can only be scored against a
crystallographic answer and no ligand of the dataset has one. Donepezil spans both ends
of the gorge, so the validation covers a pose that engages the catalytic and the
peripheral anionic site at once rather than one site alone. It was docked with five
independent seeds, and a pose was counted as successful below \SI{2.0}{\angstrom} RMSD
from the crystal pose, which is the conventional criterion. The crystal pose was
recovered at $0.745 \pm 0.009$~\AA{} in five of five runs, as Figure~\ref{fig:redock}
shows, clearing that criterion by nearly a factor of three. The spread of
\SI{0.009}{\angstrom} across the five seeds shows the recovery to be a property of the
protocol rather than a fortunate draw.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{Figure2_redocking_validation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Redocking validation.} Donepezil redocked into 4EY7, its own
    structure, against the crystal pose, with Trp86 and Tyr337 marked.}
  \label{fig:redock}
\end{figure}

The pose-geometry and molecular-dynamics descriptors are defined in terms of residue
contacts and not of RMSD, so we validated the protocol a second time at the level of
interactions. Interaction fingerprints were computed for the crystal
pose and the redocked pose of donepezil by using ProLIF \citep{prolif2021}, with bond orders
transferred from the docked structure so that the two poses carry identical chemistry
and differ only in their coordinates. The crystal binding mode is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:interactions}(a), and Figure~\ref{fig:interactions}(b) sets the two
fingerprints side by side, with every interaction placed by the residue it involves and
by its type. Counting residues alone understates what changes, since a residue
contacted in both poses can still gain or lose an interaction, as Tyr341 does. Counted
interaction by interaction, the crystal pose carries 18 and the redocked pose 14, of
which 11 are common, 7 appear only in the crystal pose and 3 only in the docked one.
Ten of the 18 crystal interactions are typed rather than bare van der Waals contact,
and 8 of those 10 are recovered, namely the hydrophobic and $\pi$-stacking contacts at
Trp86 and at Trp286 and the cation--$\pi$ and hydrophobic contacts at Tyr337. The two
typed interactions that are lost are a hydrogen-bond acceptor contact with Phe295 and a
hydrophobic contact with Phe297, and neither lies at one of the three named sites.
Every remaining difference is van der Waals contact, lost at Tyr72, Ser203, Ser293 and
His447 and gained at Tyr124, Tyr341 and Gly448. The three sites on which the descriptors
of this work rest are therefore reproduced in full, and the disagreement is confined to
the weakest class of contact and to residues outside them. We used the typing for validation only, and it enters no descriptor set.

    the colour of each edge gives the type of interaction. (b)~The two fingerprints

\subsection{Stability and thermodynamic state of the simulations}\label{sec:simqc}

The production ensembles ran at the thermodynamic state they were set to, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:thermo}. The temperature in panel~(a) averages \SI{300.00}{\kelvin} with a run-to-run spread of
\SI{0.02}{\kelvin}, and the density in panel~(b) averages
\SI{1018.9}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed} with a spread of
\SI{0.12}{\kilo\gram\per\metre\cubed}. The potential energy in panel~(c) drifts by
\SI{-10.5}{\kilo\joule\per\mole\per\nano\second} at the median, against a total near
$-1.3\times10^{6}$~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole}, which is two parts in $10^{5}$ per
nanosecond. Panel~(d) shows the running mean of the pressure, which ends at
\SI{1.00}{\bar}, since the instantaneous pressure of a box of this size has a
within-run spread of about \SI{89}{\bar} and carries no information frame by frame.
These four quantities were computed on 64 of the 229 runs, and the subset establishes
only that the simulations were well behaved, no comparison between descriptor sets
resting on it. What the four panels establish is that the integrator, the thermostat
and the barostat behaved, and a drift of two parts in $10^{5}$ per nanosecond rules
out a systematic leak of energy.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_thermo.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Production ensembles ran at the intended thermodynamic state.}
    (a)~Temperature, (b)~density, (c)~potential energy and (d)~the running mean of
    the pressure, each drawn as the median across runs with the 5--95 percentile
    band. Panel~(d) shows a running mean rather than the instantaneous pressure,
    which fluctuates by hundreds of bar from frame to frame.}
  \label{fig:thermo}
\end{figure}

Structural stability across the campaign is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stability}, where
the receptor C$\alpha$ RMSD in panel~(a) has a median of \SI{1.42}{\angstrom} across
runs and reaches \SI{2.20}{\angstrom} at worst. The steep rise in that panel is
complete within the five nanoseconds that are discarded, and the curve creeps only
slowly over the window that is analysed, so the descriptors are not measured on a
system still finding its equilibrium. The ligand RMSD from its equilibrated pose in
panel~(b) has a median of \SI{2.99}{\angstrom}, and the two production designs are
drawn separately instead of being averaged into a single band whose composition would
change partway along its own axis. They track each other over the window they share,
at \SI{1.41}{\angstrom} against \SI{1.44}{\angstrom} for the receptor and
\SI{3.06}{\angstrom} against \SI{2.84}{\angstrom} for the ligand, so the runs that
continued are not a different population and the common window may be read as one set.
A ligand displacement of three \AA{} is a rearrangement within the site and not a
departure from it, as the centre of mass of no ligand in any of the 229 runs ever moved
more than \SI{12}{\angstrom} from where it started.

The fluctuation in panel~(c) is concentrated where the receptor was cut. Its eight
largest values belong to residues 494, 541, 493, 258, 540, 4, 492 and 539, reaching
\SI{4.53}{\angstrom} at residue 494, and every one of them is a capped segment
terminus or its immediate neighbour. That is the expected behaviour of a free chain
end and it is confined to the surface, since the fourteen gorge-lining residues
fluctuate by a median of \SI{0.49}{\angstrom} and by no more than
\SI{0.75}{\angstrom}. Splitting the chain into three segments therefore left the
binding site as rigid as the rest of the fold, which is the condition the contact
descriptors depend on. All three panels were computed after the same periodic-boundary
treatment that the descriptors receive.

Taken together the two figures establish that the systems are equilibrated and
structurally stable over the window that is analysed, which is the condition the
descriptors need, and they do not establish that the sampling has converged. A
temperature and a density settle within picoseconds of any production run, and an RMSD
plateaus just as convincingly for a ligand that entered one basin early and stayed in
it. The replicate pilot measures that directly, by running the same ligand again under a
different velocity seed. The share of descriptors whose seed-to-seed spread swamps
their spread between ligands does not fall as the window lengthens, standing at 19 of
32 at \SI{5}{\nano\second}, 21 at \SI{20}{\nano\second} and 20 at
\SI{45}{\nano\second}. A converging simulation would show that share shrinking, and it
does not. What these two figures rule out is therefore a system that is drifting,
unfolding or losing its ligand, which is a weaker statement than convergence and the
one the descriptors actually require.

\subsection{Comparison of the four descriptor sets}\label{sec:ladder}

Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} gives the best score each rung reached on each framing,
together with the algorithm family that reached it. On \framingA{} the 2D
fingerprints lead at $+0.386$, with the
receptor ensemble second at $+0.215$, MM/GBSA at $+0.118$, molecular dynamics at
$+0.068$ and the single rigid pose last at $+0.046$. On \framingB{}, the endpoint that defines a cliff, the order changes, as the receptor ensemble reaches $+0.274$ against
$+0.200$ for the fingerprints, molecular dynamics reaches $+0.152$, pose geometry
$+0.167$ and MM/GBSA falls below zero at $-0.056$. On \framingC{} every rung separates cliff pairs from controls better than chance, the fingerprints leading at $0.805$ and
molecular dynamics close behind at $0.796$, with MM/GBSA at $0.753$, the ensemble at
$0.705$ and the single pose at $0.684$. Every rung was scored on all 217 development
ligands and all 145 development pairs, so no rung is helped by an easier subset of the
data, and the winning algorithm family differs between cells rather than one family
carrying a rung throughout.

    arms and the algorithm families, on 217 development ligands and 145 development
    pairs. The winning family is named in parentheses. The full grid is
    the supplementary tables at the end of this section.}
  \label{tab:benchmark}
  \input{../results/22_benchmark_main.tex}
\end{table}

No rung is resolvably ahead of any other on any framing, and the ordering above should
be read with that in mind. The largest advantage any structure-based rung holds over
the fingerprints anywhere is the $0.074$ by which the receptor ensemble leads on
\framingB{}, and the largest lead the fingerprints hold over the best structure-based
rung is $0.171$ on \framingA{} and $0.010$ on \framingC{}. All three are well inside
the resolution of about $0.3$ that this design can claim, so none of them is a
difference it can see. The claim is therefore not that two-dimensional
fingerprints win everywhere, since on \framingB{} the ensemble is numerically ahead of
them, but that no rung is separated from any other by more than the noise. The one
comparison that does separate is the gap between rung~4 and the fingerprints on
\framingA{}, and it runs against rung~4.

The headline comparison gives rung~1 thousands of features and rung~4 twelve, so the
obvious objection is that the fingerprint wins on width rather than on content. We
therefore scored an arm in which the fingerprint is cut to the width of rung~4 by a
univariate filter fitted inside each training fold. The width is twelve features on
framings~A and~B and twenty-four on \framingC{}, where each pair contributes a
concatenation of two vectors. The control does not settle the question in one
direction, as it reverses the ordering on two framings and leaves it standing on the
third. On \framingB{} the handicapped fingerprint falls to $-0.128$ from $+0.200$ and
so drops below the $+0.152$ of rung~4, and on \framingC{} it falls to $0.713$ from
$0.805$ and below the $0.796$ of rung~4. On \framingA{} it falls only to $+0.165$ from
$+0.386$ and remains well above the $+0.068$ of rung~4. The two framings whose ordering changes are
the two the design cannot resolve, and the one framing it can resolve is the one left
unchanged.

We read that as a statement about the control rather than as evidence for rung~4,
because the two sides are not handicapped symmetrically. The twelve rung-4 features are
what a reliability criterion left of a set designed for this target, whereas the twelve
fingerprint bits are what a univariate filter left of two thousand that carry their
information in combination rather than singly. Cutting a fingerprint to twelve bits
does not produce a smaller fingerprint, it produces a different and much poorer
descriptor. The arm therefore shows that feature count is not the quantity on which
this comparison turns, which is worth establishing but is not a result about molecular
dynamics. The comparison that matters is therefore against rung~1 as it would
actually be used, with every feature it has and the feature set re-selected inside each
bootstrap replicate.

Against that unhandicapped baseline, and as Figure~\ref{fig:gapforest} shows, the
bootstrap over units puts the gap at $-0.375$ on \framingA{} with a 95 per cent interval of $[-0.596, -0.184]$, at $-0.093$ on
\framingB{} with $[-0.319, +0.127]$, and at $+0.003$ on \framingC{} with $[-0.102,
+0.103]$. Only \framingA{} is resolved, and it is resolved against rung~4 rather than for it.
That interval is the only one in this study that excludes zero, and its sign matters as much
as its width, since it does not report rung~4 as unproven but as worse than a
fingerprint on per-ligand potency. On the two pair framings the gap is small and the
interval contains zero in both, so neither supports a claim in either direction, and
the figure colours the three accordingly. The baseline throughout is rung~1 with every
feature it has, its feature set re-selected inside each replicate, so the gap is not
measured against a weakened fingerprint. The
molecular-dynamics descriptors therefore fail to improve on a 2D baseline where the
design can see, and leave the question open where it cannot.

\subsection{Statistical power of the comparison}\label{sec:power}

The resolution of the comparison was measured on synthetic descriptor matrices pushed
through the same support vector machine and the same frozen folds, on the pair endpoint
of \framingB{}. The baseline in that simulation is itself synthetic, built by tuning
the signal strength of a descriptor block until its out-of-fold coefficient of
determination matched the $0.200$ that rung~1 reaches, which the tuning met at $0.209$.
Figure~\ref{fig:power}(a) gives, against that baseline, the share of simulations in
which a candidate of a given strength is called the better of the two. A candidate
scoring $0.335$ is called better in 36 per cent of them and one scoring $0.479$ in 90
per cent, while 80 per cent power arrives only at about $0.45$. The curve runs along
the bottom until roughly $0.3$ and climbs steeply after it, so the design is not merely
less sensitive to a small advantage but close to blind to one. The dash-dotted line
marks where rung~4 landed, to the left of the baseline and inside that flat stretch.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3\_power.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Detection limit of the comparison, and the assay-noise ceiling.}
    (a)~Power to call a candidate descriptor set the better of the two, against a
    synthetic baseline tuned to the $0.200$ of rung~1 and re-simulated inside every
    replicate, with the dash-dotted line marking where rung~4 landed. (b)~Width of
    the 95\% interval on the gap across the same range of candidates. (c)~The ceiling
    that assay reproducibility imposes, from the 336 of 5{,}609 compounds carrying a
    repeat, against where each rung lies.}
  \label{fig:power}
\end{figure}

Panel~(b) carries the same limit as a width rather than as a probability. The 95 per
cent interval on the gap is $0.32$ wide on average and stays between $0.28$ and $0.34$
across every candidate tested, so the limit barely depends on how strong the candidate
is. The portable statement is therefore that this study separates two values of the
coefficient of determination about $0.3$ apart, and not less. That number was fixed before the comparison it bounds had been scored, so it describes
the design rather than the outcome. It bounds the null rather than leaving it open, and
both halves of the bound deserve stating. Differences larger than about $0.3$ are excluded, so the claim
that molecular dynamics adds a large advantage over fingerprints is ruled out on this
dataset. Differences smaller than that are not addressed at all, so a modest advantage
remains possible and this study cannot speak to it. What would change the bound is the
number of pairs rather than the length of the trajectories, since the width follows
from 145 pairs.

Panel~(c) closes the one remaining way of reading the result, which is that the
endpoint itself is too noisy to predict. A model cannot explain more of the variance
than the measurement reproduces, and we estimated that ceiling from the 336 of 5609
compounds carrying a repeat assay. Each compound's spread across its repeats gives a
noise variance, and the ceiling is the share of the observed variance that a perfect
model could still account for. The panel draws that ceiling for three readings of the spread, whose median is
$0.06$ log units, whose mean is $0.18$ and whose upper decile is $0.56$, and shades
the variance above it as unreachable. Even the pessimistic reading taken from the
upper decile leaves every rung of the ladder far below the shaded band. The variance the ladder fails
to explain is therefore real signal and not assay noise, which places the limitation in
the descriptors rather than in the data, and removes the reading that there was nothing
there to find.

\subsection{Reliability of the dynamic descriptors}\label{sec:reliability}
Which descriptors a trajectory can carry at all was settled by the replicate pilot, and
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}(a) shows the ratio it measures for every descriptor at
every analysis window. Of the 160 combinations of descriptor and window, 60 clear the
threshold of 2 and 100 do not, and the failures are not spread evenly over the three
design classes. Persistence quantities clear it in 53 of 100 combinations, fluctuation
quantities in 3 of 35, and event counts in 4 of 20, which panel~(b) gives as the
composition of verdicts at each window. The remaining five combinations are the
receptor backbone RMSD, which is carried as a quality-control quantity rather than as
a member of any design class and never clears the threshold. The fraction of the window over which a
contact is held is therefore reproducible across velocity seeds, while the amplitude of
motion within that window, at this trajectory length, largely is not. The threshold itself was
fixed before any replicate had been scored, so the division is not a line drawn around
the answer.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig4_reliability.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Reliability of each descriptor against the replicate test.}
    (a)~Ratio of the spread across ligands to the spread across replicates of the same
    ligand, for each descriptor at each analysis window, against the threshold of 2
    fixed before the pilot ran. (b)~Verdict composition by window, with the
    descriptors grouped by design class.}
  \label{fig:reliability}
\end{figure}

The event counts behave as a class only in the sense that three of their four members
never survive. Every one of the four combinations that does is the rate of bridging
episodes, which clears the threshold at 5, 10, 20 and \SI{45}{\nano\second}. It fails
only at \SI{30}{\nano\second}, the one window where the between-ligand spread rests on
45 ligands rather than on all 217, so that failure reflects the sample rather than the
descriptor. The longest bridging episode is dominated by the seed at every length, the
Tyr337 rotamer count is either too sparse to assess or seed-dominated, and whether the
ligand left the gorge is constant because no ligand ever did. A rate can therefore
survive a replicate test, but only where the events it counts are frequent enough to be
counted reliably in the window available.

At the \SI{20}{\nano\second} window that production uses, 11 of the 32 descriptors
pass, and 9 of those are contact occupancies. That concentration is the practical
finding of this subsection and the one least tied to this target. Anyone building
descriptors from trajectories of this length should expect persistence-type observables
to survive a replicate test and fluctuation-type observables not to, and should measure
that rather than assume it. The filter also earns its place within this study rather
than remaining a diagnostic, since the filtered set reaches $+0.152$ on \framingB{}
against $-0.054$ for the unfiltered one and $0.796$ on \framingC{} against $0.721$.
Removing the descriptors that measure the seed therefore improves what remains on both
pair endpoints, which is what a filter of this kind is supposed to do and is not
guaranteed to achieve.

\subsection{Interpretation of the distance-based contact occupancies}\label{sec:typed}

Two of the eleven descriptors that survive the replicate test are \texttt{occ\_cas}
and \texttt{occ\_pas}, which count the fraction of frames in which a ligand heavy atom
lies within \SI{4.5}{\angstrom} of Trp86 or Trp286. That criterion is geometric and
says nothing about whether a chemical interaction is present, so we recomputed typed
interaction fingerprints over the trajectories with ProLIF \citep{prolif2021} and
compared the two. Figure~\ref{fig:dyninteractions}(a) gives the median occupancy of
each type at each gorge-lining residue. Each cell is a median over the runs in which that
interaction is detected, so it describes how persistent an interaction is when it forms
rather than how often it forms. The typing was computed on 21 frames of each run, which
resolves an occupancy to about five percentage points, so differences smaller than that
should not be read. Hydrophobic engagement at Trp86 and at Trp286 occupies
the whole window at the median, and it is detected in 91 and 92 per cent of runs.
$\pi$-stacking reaches 70 and 57 per cent of the window, in the 76 and 79 per cent of
runs that show it at all. The cation--$\pi$ contact that these cationic ligands were expected
to form is the weakest of the three on both counts, holding 43 per cent of the window
at Trp86 and 38 at Trp286, and appearing in 58 and 29 per cent of runs. Eight of the 229 runs could not be typed because
ProLIF reported a fragmented residue, so the panel rests on 221.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_dynamic_interactions.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Typed interaction occupancy, and what the distance-only
    descriptors summarise.} (a)~Median occupancy of each interaction type at each
    gorge-lining residue, taken over the runs in which that interaction is detected
    rather than over all 221, with cells left blank where it is detected in fewer than
    a tenth of them. The typing was computed on 21 frames of each run, 46 for the runs
    that were continued, so a cell resolves occupancy to about five percentage points. (b)~The typed occupancy of any type at
    Trp86 and Trp286 against the distance-only \texttt{occ\_cas} and \texttt{occ\_pas}
    of this work, one point per run, against the diagonal on which the two would
    agree.}
  \label{fig:dyninteractions}
\end{figure}

Panel~(b) places the typed occupancy against the distance-only one, run by run. The two
are not computed on the same frames, since the distance-only occupancy uses all 2000
frames of the window while the typing uses 21, so a small disagreement in either
direction is expected and a few points sit above the diagonal. The Spearman coefficients of $0.61$ at
Trp86 and $0.55$ at Trp286 overstate the agreement, because both measures sit at 100
per cent for 77 per cent of runs at Trp86 and 56 per cent at Trp286, so most of the
coefficient is ties. Recomputed on the runs where at least one measure is below
saturation, they fall to $0.39$ and $0.22$. The informative statistic is the signed
difference, which has a median of zero but reaches $+75$ and $+77$ percentage points.
There are therefore runs in which the ligand sits within \SI{4.5}{\angstrom} of the
tryptophan for nearly every frame while ProLIF recognises no interaction there at all.

The disagreement is not evidence that one measure is right and the other wrong, since
neither is a ground truth and the typed criteria are thresholds of their own. The
distance-only descriptors measure what they are defined to measure, which is proximity,
and they measure it reproducibly. What the panel shows is that proximity saturates,
so for the ligands that are not simply pinned against the site, which are exactly the
ones a cliff comparison turns on, the descriptor returns the same value whatever the
chemistry is doing. That bears on the reliability result above, because a quantity
sitting at 100 per cent in most runs is reproducible for a reason that has nothing to
do with its usefulness. The filter of Section~\ref{sec:reliability} will therefore
favour a saturated quantity over a discriminating one, which is a property of that
filter worth stating rather than a fault in these descriptors.

The typed occupancy is not a ready replacement for it either, and the same replicate
test says why. Put through that test, it gives a ratio of $2.63$ at Trp86 and $1.05$ at Trp286, against $103$ and $52$
for the distance-only descriptors, so one of the two would not pass the filter of
Section~\ref{sec:reliability} at all. Part of that noise is the coarser sampling, and
a typing computed on every frame might fare better. The exchange is therefore
discrimination against reproducibility rather than chemistry against geometry, and
this work has tested neither side of it as a predictor. Nothing in this subsection
enters any model, since a typed descriptor family introduced after the ladder had been
scored would be post hoc, so it is left as a question for a later study.

\subsection{Dependence on trajectory length}\label{sec:duration}

Whether \SI{20}{\nano\second} is longer than the descriptors need was tested by
recomputing them on the first 5, 10, 20 and \SI{30}{\nano\second} of each trajectory
and scoring each window on the frozen folds. Two series are drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:duration}, because the replicate filter selects a different set at each
window, 14 features at 5 and \SI{10}{\nano\second} against 12 at 20, and letting it do
so would confound how much trajectory a descriptor saw with which descriptors were
chosen. The fixed series holds the \SI{20}{\nano\second} selection at every window and
is the one that isolates length. On it, \framingA{} in panel~(a) moves between $+0.030$ and
$+0.044$, \framingB{} in panel~(b) between $+0.149$ and $+0.196$, and \framingC{} in
panel~(c) between $0.747$ and $0.796$. Each of those spreads is smaller than the
interval the corresponding framing carries, so none of them is a dependence this design
can resolve.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5\_duration.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Trajectory length is not the limiting factor between 5 and
    20~ns.} Panels show (a)~\framingA{}, (b)~\framingB{} and (c)~\framingC{}, with
    descriptors recomputed on the first 5, 10, 20 and 30~ns of analysed
    trajectory. The per-window replicate filter gives 14, 14 and 12 features at 5, 10
    and 20~ns and does not select the same ones at each, so it confounds trajectory
    length with descriptor choice; the fixed series holds the 20~ns selection at every
    window and isolates length.
    Only the 45 development ligands whose runs were continued past production reach
    30~ns, drawn as their own series and never joined to the full-sample line.}
  \label{fig:duration}
\end{figure}

The \SI{30}{\nano\second} point is drawn apart because only the 45 development ligands
whose runs were continued reach it, and on \framingA{} it rests on 11 pairs rather than
145. It is shown so that the curve does not appear to stop for want of data, and it
should not be read as a fall at longer trajectories, since the sample changes with it.
Those 45 are the ligands whose runs happened to be continued rather than a random draw
from the 217, so the two series are not interchangeable even at the windows they share. Nothing in the three panels supports the
view that the simulations were too short, which is the first objection a null result
from molecular dynamics attracts. What they establish between them is that trajectory
length is not the lever. The descriptors are no better
at \SI{20}{\nano\second} than at 10, and the practical reading is that they can be
computed from \SI{15}{\nano\second} of simulation rather than the 50 the original design
called for, which answers the cost objection with a measurement rather than an argument.

One number in these panels should not be quoted on its own. The $+0.196$ that
\framingB{} reaches at \SI{10}{\nano\second} is the best of six cells computed together,
and reporting it as the performance of molecular dynamics at 10 nanoseconds would be
exactly the selection this paper exists to warn against. The spread across the three
windows is the quantity to read, not its maximum. What would move the result is
not the length of the trajectories but the number of pairs, since the resolution of
about $0.3$ follows from 145 of them. More nanoseconds do not change it, and more pairs
or more targets are the only things that would.

\subsection{Effect of an incomplete subset on the apparent gain}
\label{sec:subset}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{The gain reported at 59 of 145 pairs was an artefact worth
    $+0.277$.} Prefixes follow the real completion order, recovered from each
    run's file timestamp; random subsets of the same ligand count are the control.
