\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Activity cliffs are pairs of near-identical compounds separated by orders of magnitude
in potency. They are the standard counter-example to quantitative structure-activity
relationships, and the usual response is to add physical detail that a fingerprint
cannot see. However, it is rarely tested against a ligand-based baseline on the same
data, and none reports what difference its design can detect. We escalate four structure-based representations against one fingerprint baseline on
human acetylcholinesterase: a docked pose, an eight-member receptor ensemble, an
implicit-solvent energy, and descriptors from \SI{25}{\nano\second} of molecular dynamics
run for every ligand. The ladder is scored on 217 ligands and 145 pairs, 77 constitutional
cliffs against 68 matched controls, on three endpoint framings. Splits are frozen and
the detection limit measured in advance, at $R^2$ about $0.3$. On the development split
we find that no structure-based representation is resolvably better than the
fingerprint on any framing, and that the dynamic descriptors are resolvably worse on
per-ligand potency. We then opened the sealed test split once, and it does not agree. On the signed pair
difference the receptor ensemble and the dynamic descriptors lead the
fingerprint by $+0.283$ and $+0.311$, and both gaps survive a correction for twelve
comparisons and a leave-one-pair-out check. Thirty-eight held-out pairs against 145 cannot settle which reading is right. That instability is itself the
result, and it bounds what any single frozen split of this size can establish. What would settle the question is more pairs and targets, not more
nanoseconds.
\end{abstract}
