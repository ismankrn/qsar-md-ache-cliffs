\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Activity cliffs, pairs of near-identical compounds separated by orders of magnitude in
potency, are the standard counter-example to quantitative structure-activity
relationships, and the standard remedy is a representation carrying detail a
fingerprint cannot. That remedy is rarely tested against a ligand-based baseline on the
same data, and no study reports how large a difference its design could detect. We
escalated four structure-based representations against one fingerprint baseline on
human acetylcholinesterase, from the geometry of a docked pose through an eight-member
receptor ensemble and an implicit-solvent energy to descriptors from
\SI{25}{\nano\second} molecular dynamics trajectories. The ladder was scored on 271
ligands and 145 matched pairs, 77 of them constitutional cliffs, on three framings of
the endpoint, with splits frozen and the detection limit measured in advance. No
structure-based representation was resolvably better than the fingerprint on any
framing, the design separating coefficients of determination about $0.3$ apart. On
per-ligand potency the dynamics descriptors were resolvably worse, the gap being
$-0.375$ with a 95 per cent interval of $[-0.596, -0.184]$, while both pair endpoints
gave intervals containing zero. The unexplained variance lies far beyond assay
reproducibility and does not move with trajectory length, placing the limitation in the
descriptors rather than the data. A replicate test found that such a trajectory
reproduces how long a contact is held, in 53 of 100 descriptor and window combinations,
but the amplitude of motion in only 3 of 35. What would settle the question left open
is more pairs and targets, not more nanoseconds.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Activity cliff; quantitative structure-activity
relationship; molecular dynamics descriptor; ensemble docking; acetylcholinesterase;
detection limit
