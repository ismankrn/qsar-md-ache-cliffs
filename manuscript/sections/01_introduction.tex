\section{Introduction}

An activity cliff is a pair of compounds that a chemist would call near-identical and
an assay separates by orders of magnitude in potency. Such pairs are the standard
counter-example to quantitative structure-activity relationships, since a model built
on molecular similarity predicts similar potencies for similar structures and a cliff
is the case where that inference fails by construction. The failure is documented
rather than anecdotal. \citet{vantilborg2022} scored 24 machine-learning methods over
30 targets and found descriptor-based models outperforming deep learning on cliff
pairs, while \citet{dablander2023} examined nine QSAR models on three targets and
reported that they fail on cliffs often enough to matter, with the
extended-connectivity fingerprint remaining the strongest representation tested.

The standard explanation for that failure is representational. A two-dimensional
fingerprint encodes which substructures a molecule contains, so two molecules differing
by a single substituent share almost all of their bits, and whatever that substituent
does to the three-dimensional fit, to the hydrogen bonding or to the residence time in
the site is invisible to it. The remedy that follows is equally standard, which is to
replace or augment the fingerprint with a representation carrying the physics. That
reasoning is plausible, widely repeated, and rarely tested in the form in which it is
stated. Testing it requires three things at once, namely a structure-based
representation scored on a cliff endpoint, a ligand-based baseline scored on the same
splits and the same folds, and a statement of how large a difference the design could
have detected had one been there.

The closest precedent supplies the first of those three and not the other two.
\citet{husby2015} reports that activity cliffs can be predicted accurately by advanced
structure-based methods, and the numbers behind that statement repay reading. Template
docking reaches a median accuracy of 90 per cent, using the crystallographic pose of
the more potent member of the pair. A pocket ensemble selected with the cliff labels
themselves reaches 71 to 100 per cent, which those authors describe as a purely
retrospective exercise. The prospective arm, cross-docking, sits at a median of 53 per
cent on a two-way ranking task. Both members of every pair in that benchmark carry a
crystal structure, the endpoint is pairwise ranking rather than a signed difference,
and no ligand-based comparison is run anywhere in the paper. What this leaves open is
the prospective setting with one receptor, a harder endpoint, and a matched
two-dimensional baseline.

This study occupies that opening by escalation. Four structure-based representations of
increasing physical detail were built for the same ligands and scored against the same
fingerprint baseline, running from the geometry of a single docked pose, through the
same geometry measured over an eight-member receptor ensemble, to an implicit-solvent
interaction energy, and finally to descriptors extracted from molecular dynamics
trajectories of \SI{25}{\nano\second}. The target is human acetylcholinesterase, which
supplies 271 ligands and 145 matched pairs, 77 of them constitutional activity cliffs.
Each representation met three framings of the same data, namely per-ligand potency, the
signed potency difference within a pair, and the separation of cliff pairs from
controls. The splits were frozen before any descriptor was computed and a test set was
sealed. What the comparison could have detected was measured on synthetic descriptor
matrices pushed through the same models and the same folds, and was stated before the
comparison it bounds had been scored.

Little of the descriptor machinery is new, and this paper does not claim otherwise.
Residue-wise interaction fingerprints date to \citet{sift2004}, \citet{prolif2021}
computes them from molecular dynamics trajectories, and \citet{ifp_md_2024} aggregates
them over a trajectory into occupancies. The contribution is the application of such
descriptors to activity cliffs against a matched baseline with a stated detection
limit, rather than the descriptors themselves.

Five contributions follow. A ladder of five descriptor sets is scored on three framings
of one well-characterised target with frozen splits, and no structure-based rung is
resolvably better than a two-dimensional fingerprint on any of them. The detection
limit of that comparison is measured rather than assumed, which bounds the null instead
of leaving it open. The variance the ladder leaves unexplained is placed beyond what
assay reproducibility accounts for and shown not to move with trajectory length, which
locates the limitation in the descriptors rather than in the data. A replicate test
establishes which dynamic quantities a trajectory of this length can carry at all,
separating persistence from amplitude. One measurement artefact is quantified, in which
a descriptor filter refitted at each value of a varied parameter manufactures a trend
that a single fixed selection removes.
