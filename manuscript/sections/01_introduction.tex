\section{Introduction}

An activity cliff is a pair of compounds that a chemist would call near-identical and
an assay separates by orders of magnitude in potency \citep{maggiora2006}. Such pairs
are the standard counter-example to quantitative structure-activity relationships. A
model built on molecular similarity predicts similar potencies for similar structures,
and a cliff is the case where that inference fails by construction. These pairs are
neither rare nor confined to awkward corners of chemical space. They are distributed
across target families and compound series, which makes them a structural feature of
bioactivity data rather than a curation problem \citep{stumpfe2012}. The failure they
cause is documented rather than anecdotal. \citet{vantilborg2022} have scored 24
machine-learning methods over 30 targets. They found that descriptor-based models
outperform deep learning on cliff pairs. \citet{dablander2023} have examined nine QSAR
models on three targets. They reported that these models fail on cliffs often enough to
matter, and that the extended-connectivity fingerprint remains the strongest
representation tested.

Predicting cliffs matters more than their frequency alone would suggest, because a
cliff is where a medicinal chemistry programme spends its effort. A small substitution
that multiplies potency is exactly the move lead optimisation exists to find. Thus the
pairs a model handles worst are the pairs a project cares about most
\citep{stumpfe2019}. The same pairs also carry the most structure-activity information
per atom changed, which makes them the informative cases rather than the awkward ones
\citep{cruzmonteagudo2014}. Meanwhile, failure on them is quiet. A model scored over a
whole dataset can report a respectable average while being wrong on every cliff in it,
since cliffs are a minority of pairs and an aggregate metric hides them. A method can
therefore be adopted on a benchmark that never tested the case it will be used for.

The standard explanation for that failure is representational. An
extended-connectivity fingerprint encodes which circular substructures a molecule
contains \citep{rogers2010}. Two molecules differing by a single substituent thus share
almost all of their bits, and whatever that substituent does to the three-dimensional
fit, to the hydrogen bonding or to how long a contact is held is invisible to it. The
response that follows is equally standard, which is to replace or augment the
fingerprint with a representation that carries the physics. Docked pose geometry,
receptor ensembles, implicit-solvent energies and molecular dynamics are each proposed
in that spirit, and each is more expensive than the fingerprint it is meant to improve
upon.

Most of the effort has gone elsewhere. Work on cliff prediction has run almost entirely
within the two-dimensional representation, and it has escalated in model complexity
rather than in physical content. \citet{tamura2023} have taken that escalation to its
conclusion by scoring machine and deep learning methods of increasing complexity at
scale. They found that the added complexity does not buy accuracy on cliffs.
\citet{acgcn2022} have applied graph convolution directly to matched molecular pairs,
and \citet{elaiwat2025} have brought deep learning to cliffs within a single target
family. The benchmarks agree on an uncomfortable result, since the fingerprint keeps
winning \citep{vantilborg2022,dablander2023,tamura2023}. Recently, the field has
changed tack and has pursued better training objectives instead of larger models.
\citet{cui2026} have made a graph network aware of cliffs during training through a
contrastive objective. They report an improvement on both fingerprint and graph
baselines across 52 datasets. That is the current state of the art, and the trend it
completes is one of extracting more from the same representation. Every method named
here still sees a molecule as a two-dimensional object.

On the other hand, where a structure-based representation has been tested on cliffs, it
has been tested in a setting more generous than the one it would be used in.
\citet{husby2015} reports that activity cliffs can be predicted accurately by advanced
structure-based methods. The numbers behind that statement are worth stating. Template
docking reaches a median accuracy of 90 per cent by using the crystallographic pose of
the more potent member of the pair. A pocket ensemble selected with the cliff labels
themselves reaches 71 to 100 per cent, which those authors describe as a purely
retrospective exercise. The prospective arm, cross-docking, sits at a median of 53 per
cent on a two-way ranking task. In addition, both members of every pair in that
benchmark carry a crystal structure, the endpoint is pairwise ranking rather than a
signed difference, and no ligand-based comparison is run anywhere in the paper.

Molecular dynamics has been brought to a cliff-infested target before, in a different
role. \citet{abudayah2022} extracts pharmacophore models from trajectory frames of a
potent binder and selects among them by using a genetic-algorithm QSAR, then uses the
result to screen a compound collection. The trajectory there supplies the hypothesis
that a screening model is built from, and the question asked of it is whether actives
can be retrieved.

Three gaps follow, and they are gaps in the state of the art rather than oversights in
any one study. The first is representational. Work on cliff prediction has varied the
model and has left the molecule two-dimensional. The explanation everyone offers for
why cliffs are hard, that the fingerprint cannot see what separates the pair, has
therefore gone untested in that line of work, because no one there supplies what it
says is missing. The second is the
comparison itself. The structure-based work that exists runs no ligand-based baseline,
and the ligand-based work runs no structure-based one, so the two lines of evidence
have never met on the same compounds and the same splits. The third is the detection
limit. No study on either side reports how large a difference its design could have
resolved, which leaves every negative result indistinguishable from an underpowered one
and every positive result unbounded. Together these mean that the field has a
well-supported observation, that fingerprints are hard to beat on cliffs, and no
measurement of what physical detail would add to them.

Closing those gaps calls for a particular shape of experiment rather than one more
comparison. A single contest between molecular dynamics and a fingerprint cannot
distinguish physical detail failing to help from one implementation of it failing. The
representations are therefore better arranged as a ladder, in which each rung adds one
kind of information to the one below it. The rung at which an advantage appears, if any
does, then identifies what supplied it. The baseline must also be the fingerprint as it
would actually be used and scored on the same data, because a handicapped baseline
turns a comparison of representations into a comparison of feature counts. In addition,
the detection limit must be fixed before the comparison is scored, because a null
without one reports the size of the study rather than the size of the effect. Each of
these answers one of the three gaps directly.

The aim of this study is to measure what escalating physical detail adds to the
prediction of activity cliffs. In particular, we address four objectives. The first is
to measure whether structure-based representations of increasing physical detail
predict activity-cliff behaviour better than a two-dimensional fingerprint, against a
matched baseline. The second is to establish what difference such a design could have
detected, so that the outcome bounds the question rather than merely failing to answer
it. The third is to determine which dynamic quantities a trajectory of affordable
length can supply reproducibly, since a descriptor that does not survive a change of
velocity seed cannot carry an endpoint whatever its physical appeal. The fourth is to
quantify a measurement artefact met along the way, in which re-selecting descriptors at
each setting of a varied parameter manufactures a trend that a single fixed selection
removes.

Human acetylcholinesterase is the target throughout. We chose it for what such a
comparison needs rather than for being typical. Cliffs are the scarce resource here,
and this enzyme supplies them in quantity, giving 271 ligands and 183 matched pairs of
which 97 are constitutional cliffs. It is also resolved to high resolution in complex
with pharmacologically important inhibitors \citep{cheung2012}, so the structure-based
rungs rest on a crystallographic receptor of the human enzyme rather than on a homology
model. The binding site is the part that matters most for the hypothesis under test.
Ligands bind down a narrow gorge with an anionic site at each end, and spanning both is
an established design rationale rather than one invented for this study
\citep{grabowska2025}. This gives a dynamic descriptor a concrete physical quantity to
carry. The gorge is also known to gate conformationally \citep{gating1998,gorge2017},
so a single docked pose is a poor description of it on grounds that have nothing to do
with the present result. The target is therefore favourable to the hypothesis being
tested, which is what makes the outcome worth reporting. A comparison that finds no
advantage where one is most expected says more than one that finds none where none was.

The ladder runs from the geometry of a single docked pose, through the same geometry
measured over an eight-member receptor ensemble \citep{amaro2018}, to an
implicit-solvent interaction energy \citep{genheden2015}, and finally to descriptors
extracted from molecular dynamics trajectories of \SI{25}{\nano\second}. Little of that
machinery is new and this paper does not claim otherwise. Residue-wise interaction
fingerprints date to \citet{sift2004}, \citet{prolif2021} computes them from molecular
dynamics trajectories, and \citet{ifp_md_2024} aggregates them over a trajectory into
occupancies. What is offered here is the comparison these descriptors have not yet been
put to, together with the detection limit that makes its outcome readable.
