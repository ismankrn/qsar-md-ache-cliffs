\section{Introduction}

An activity cliff is a pair of compounds that a chemist would call near-identical and
an assay separates by orders of magnitude in potency \citep{maggiora2006}. Such pairs
are the standard counter-example to quantitative structure-activity relationships,
since a model built on molecular similarity predicts similar potencies for similar
structures and a cliff is the case where that inference fails by construction. They are
neither rare nor confined to awkward corners of chemical space, being distributed
across target families and compound series in a way that makes them a structural
feature of bioactivity data rather than a curation problem
\citep{stumpfe2012,cruzmonteagudo2014}. The failure they cause is documented rather
than anecdotal. \citet{vantilborg2022} scored 24 machine-learning methods over 30
targets and found descriptor-based models outperforming deep learning on cliff pairs,
while \citet{dablander2023} examined nine QSAR models on three targets and reported
that they fail on cliffs often enough to matter, with the extended-connectivity
fingerprint remaining the strongest representation tested.

The standard explanation for that failure is representational. An extended-connectivity
fingerprint encodes which circular substructures a molecule contains
\citep{rogers2010}, so two molecules differing by a single substituent share almost all
of their bits, and whatever that substituent does to the three-dimensional fit, to the
hydrogen bonding or to the residence time in the site is invisible to it. The remedy
that follows is equally standard, which is to replace or augment the fingerprint with a
representation that carries the physics. Docked pose geometry, receptor ensembles,
implicit-solvent energies and molecular dynamics are each proposed in that spirit, and
each is more expensive than the fingerprint it is meant to improve upon.

Where that remedy has been tested on cliffs, it has been tested in a setting more
generous than the one it would be used in. \citet{husby2015} reports that activity
cliffs can be predicted accurately by advanced structure-based methods, and the numbers
behind that statement repay reading. Template docking reaches a median accuracy of 90
per cent, using the crystallographic pose of the more potent member of the pair. A
pocket ensemble selected with the cliff labels themselves reaches 71 to 100 per cent,
which those authors describe as a purely retrospective exercise. The prospective arm,
cross-docking, sits at a median of 53 per cent on a two-way ranking task. Both members
of every pair in that benchmark carry a crystal structure, the endpoint is pairwise
ranking rather than a signed difference, and no ligand-based comparison is run anywhere
in the paper.

Molecular dynamics has been brought to a cliff-infested target before, in a different
role. \citet{abudayah2022} extracts pharmacophore models from trajectory frames of a
potent binder and selects among them with a genetic-algorithm QSAR, then uses the
result to screen a compound collection. The trajectory there supplies the hypothesis
that a screening model is built from, and the question asked of it is whether actives
can be retrieved.

Three gaps follow from this, and they compound. Descriptors measured on a trajectory
have not been scored against a cliff endpoint at all, so the most expensive rung of the
usual remedy is the least tested on the problem it is recommended for. Where
structure-based methods have been tested, the strong results draw on information that
a prospective application does not possess, namely the crystallographic pose of the
answer or a pocket set chosen with the labels, while the arm that withholds that
information sits near chance. No ligand-based baseline is scored on the same compounds
and the same splits anywhere in this literature, and no study states how large a
difference its design could have detected, so a result that favours physics cannot be
separated from an easier comparison and a result that does not cannot be separated from
an underpowered one.

Closing those gaps calls for a particular shape of experiment rather than one more
comparison. A single contest between molecular dynamics and a fingerprint cannot
distinguish physical detail failing to help from one implementation of it failing, so
the representations are better arranged as a ladder in which each rung adds one kind of
information to the one below it, since the rung at which an advantage appears, if any
does, identifies what supplied it. The baseline must be the fingerprint as it would
actually be used, scored on the same frozen splits and the same folds, because a
handicapped baseline turns a comparison of representations into a comparison of feature
counts. The detection limit must be measured on the design itself and fixed before the
comparison is scored, because a null without one reports the size of the study rather
than the size of the effect. Each of these answers one of the three gaps directly.

The objectives of this study follow from that shape. The first is to measure whether
structure-based representations of increasing physical detail predict activity-cliff
behaviour better than a two-dimensional fingerprint, on three framings of the same
data, against a matched baseline and on splits frozen before any descriptor was
computed. The second is to establish what difference such a design could have detected,
so that the outcome bounds the question rather than merely failing to answer it. The
third is to determine which dynamic quantities a trajectory of affordable length can
supply reproducibly, since a descriptor that does not survive a change of velocity seed
cannot carry an endpoint whatever its physical appeal. The fourth is to quantify a
measurement artefact encountered in the course of the work, in which a descriptor
filter refitted at each value of a varied parameter manufactures a trend that a single
fixed selection removes.

Human acetylcholinesterase is the target throughout, supplying 271 ligands and 145
matched pairs, 77 of them constitutional activity cliffs. The ladder runs from the
geometry of a single docked pose, through the same geometry measured over an
eight-member receptor ensemble \citep{amaro2018}, to an implicit-solvent interaction
energy \citep{genheden2015}, and finally to descriptors extracted from molecular
dynamics trajectories of \SI{25}{\nano\second}. Little of that machinery is new and
this paper does not claim otherwise, since residue-wise interaction fingerprints date
to \citet{sift2004}, \citet{prolif2021} computes them from molecular dynamics
trajectories, and \citet{ifp_md_2024} aggregates them over a trajectory into
occupancies. What is offered here is the comparison these descriptors have not yet been
put to, together with the detection limit that makes its outcome readable.
