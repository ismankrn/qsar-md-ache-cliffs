\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset, activity-cliff definition and data splitting}\label{sec:dataset}

This work is concerned with activity cliffs, which are pairs of compounds that are
near-identical in structure and yet far apart in potency. All of the bioactivity data
used here were taken from ChEMBL \citep{chembl}, which records measured potencies
against individual protein targets. They were retrieved from its web services at
release ChEMBL~37. The target studied here is human acetylcholinesterase (hAChE), whose
ChEMBL target identifier is CHEMBL220. The enzyme hydrolyses acetylcholine, and its
inhibition raises the synaptic concentration of this neurotransmitter. This is the
basis of the cholinergic treatment of Alzheimer's disease. We selected hAChE because it
met three entry criteria, which are at least 80 compounds surviving curation, a potency
range of at least 3 log units, and at least 25 constitutional activity cliffs.

Both halves of the cliff definition need a threshold, one for how alike the two members
are and one for how far apart their potencies lie. Similarity was computed as the
Tanimoto coefficient on binary ECFP4, which are Morgan fingerprints of radius 2 folded
to 2{,}048 bits. A pair was treated as near-identical at a coefficient of at least
0.90. It was treated as a cliff when its two members also differed by at least 2.0 log
units in pChEMBL, which is a hundredfold difference in potency. Pairs were further
divided according to whether their two members differ in constitution or only in
stereochemistry. This was decided by comparing their canonical SMILES with the
stereochemistry stripped, and only the constitutional pairs were treated as
informative. ECFP is blind to stereochemistry. Thus a stereo-blind fingerprint cannot
resolve a stereoisomer pair at all, while any method that uses three-dimensional
coordinates resolves it trivially.

The dataset itself was then built in three steps, which are the curation of the
activity data, the identification of the cliff pairs, and the assembly of the pairs
followed by their splitting into a development and a sealed test set. The activity data
of hAChE were retrieved first, and only records from binding assays were kept. A record
also had to report IC\textsubscript{50}, $K_\mathrm{i}$ or $K_\mathrm{d}$ with an exact
relation in nanomolar units, to carry a pChEMBL value, to be free of ChEMBL's own
data-validity flags and not to be marked a potential duplicate. A single assay type was
then kept, which is the most frequent one and is IC\textsubscript{50} here. Mixing
assay types is unsafe, because a pair formed from an IC\textsubscript{50} and a
$K_\mathrm{i}$ measurement can satisfy a cliff criterion by virtue of the assay alone.
Structures were standardised by stripping salts, keeping the largest covalent fragment
and neutralising formal charges from the deposited representation, and those below six
heavy atoms were removed. Replicate measurements of one standardised structure were
collapsed to their median. A structure whose replicates disagreed by more than one log
unit in standard deviation was discarded instead of being averaged, because a compound
measured inconsistently can otherwise enter a cliff pair on the strength of its own
measurement error. The curated set holds 5{,}609 compounds spanning pChEMBL 4.00 to
10.96. Within it, 421 pairs cleared the similarity criterion with a difference of at
least one log unit, 110 of them reached the hundredfold criterion, and 100 of those
were constitutional.

The compound pool was then filtered for simulation feasibility, and a compound was kept
only when it met five conditions. It had to be composed of the elements H, C, N, O, F,
S, Cl, Br, I and P, and to carry no radical electrons. It also had to have a molecular
weight between 150 and 700~Da, at most 55 heavy atoms and at most 18 rotatable bonds,
and to be embeddable in three dimensions by using ETKDGv3. This removed 239 of the
5{,}609 compounds, most of them on molecular weight or on rotatable-bond count. We
applied the filter to the whole pool before any pairing, because a pair is lost as soon
as either of its members is removed. Filtering first therefore keeps every surviving
pair intact by construction. Of the 100 constitutional cliff pairs, 97 survived with
both members intact, formed from 99 distinct compounds.

A control pair was near-identical under the same floor of 0.90, but its two members
differed by at most 0.30 log units, which is less than twofold. The two groups then
differ in the property under test and in as little else as possible. Stereoisomer pairs
were excluded here on the same grounds. The controls were drawn to reproduce the joint
distribution of similarity and mean potency of the cliff pairs over four similarity and
five potency bins, by using each control compound once. The procedure yielded 86
control pairs from 172 compounds, and the matching is close on both quantities. The
median Tanimoto coefficient is 1.000 for the cliff pairs against 0.993 for the
controls, and the mean pair-mean potency 6.64 against 6.58. Meanwhile, the quantity
under test differs by construction at 2.66 log units against 0.13. The final set holds
271 ligands and 183 pairs, which are 97 cliff and 86 control pairs. Each ligand was
prepared and simulated once, while a pair refers to two ligands, because a compound may
belong to more than one pair. Each ligand was prepared in two forms. The standardised
neutral structure carries the fingerprints and the pair definition. The dominant
protonation state at pH 7.4, assigned by explicit rules, is the one that enters the
docking and the simulations. Every ligand carries a net positive charge in that second
form, with 170 at $+1$ and 101 at $+2$.

Every cliff pair in the final set is constitutional. The two members differ in
molecular formula in all 97, and the smallest difference in heavy-atom count is one. No
result in this work therefore rests on a pair that a stereo-blind fingerprint is barred
from resolving. The fingerprint nevertheless fails to separate pairs that did enter the
set. Binary ECFP4 reported a Tanimoto coefficient of exactly 1.000 for 49 of the 97
cliff pairs, whose two members differ by a median of 4 and by as many as 8 heavy atoms,
and in potency by a median of 2.51 log units. Count-based ECFP4 separated those same 49
pairs at a median coefficient of 0.878, as Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}(a) shows. Thus
folding counts to bits discards the multiplicity that distinguishes a molecule from its
longer homologue. This bears on the selection and not only on the descriptors, because
the similarity recorded at selection agrees exactly with the audited binary coefficient
over all 97 pairs. Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}(b) places the pairs in the plane of the
definition. The empty band between the cliff pairs above 2.0 log units and the controls
below 0.30 is imposed by the criterion rather than by the chemistry. The two groups
also differ in size, by 4.4 heavy atoms on average within a cliff pair against 1.8
within a control, which Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}(c) shows and which
Section~\ref{sec:eval} adjusts for.

This set was then divided once into a development and a test part, with the random seed
being 20260902, and was never revised afterwards. The unit of assignment is not the
ligand but the connected component of the graph whose vertices are the ligands and
whose edges are the pairs. Every ligand belongs to at least one pair, and a split that
separated a pair would let a model memorise the shared scaffold from the training side.
We therefore handled the 271 ligands as the 110 components that they form. These were
stratified by whether they contain a cliff pair and by their mean potency, and 20 per
cent of each stratum was assigned to the test split. The remainder were distributed
over five cross-validation folds balanced jointly on cliff pairs and on ligand count,
because either quantity alone leaves the other badly skewed. Table~\ref{tab:splits}
gives the result. No pair has its two members on opposite sides of the test boundary or
of any fold boundary, which was verified on the written splits. The test split was held
closed while every descriptor set and algorithm was selected, and it was opened once
afterwards. Section~\ref{sec:testsplit} reports that single scoring pass, and no other
result in this work is scored on it.

\subsection{Three framings of the prediction problem}\label{sec:framings}

A comparison between descriptor sets has no single answer, because what counts as an
improvement depends on what is being predicted. Activity cliffs are the standard
counter-example to QSAR for exactly this reason. Models that predict potency well
across a series are reported to fail on the near-identical pairs within it
\citep{vantilborg2022, dablander2023}. We therefore scored every descriptor set on
three framings of the same data. These are the potency of a single ligand, the signed
difference in potency within a pair, and whether a pair is a cliff or a matched
control. They are different functions of the same descriptors, so a result on one does
not determine a result on another. In particular, separating cliff pairs from control
pairs does not require predicting the size of the difference within a pair. All three
were scored on the folds of Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and with the same algorithms,
which are described in Section~\ref{sec:algo}. The three results therefore differ in
the question being asked and in nothing else. Table~\ref{tab:framings} sets them side
by side, where $\mathbf{d}_a$ and $\mathbf{d}_b$ are the descriptor vectors of the two
ligands of a pair and the number in parentheses is the count of units available in the
development split.

    the features of one row, and the metric.}
  \label{tab:framings}
  \input{../results/31_framings.tex}
\end{table}

In framing~A the unit is the ligand, the target is its pChEMBL value, and the metric is
the coefficient of determination over the out-of-fold predictions. The predictions of
the five folds are pooled before the metric is computed, so a fold that happens to be
easy cannot be averaged against one that is hard. This is ordinary potency prediction,
which is the task on which the QSAR literature is calibrated. We included it for two
reasons. A descriptor set that cannot predict potency at all is no candidate for
predicting a difference in potency. In addition, a result on this task can be placed
beside published models in a way that a pairwise result cannot.

In framing~B the unit is the pair. The target is the signed difference in pChEMBL
between its two members, which is the quantity that defines an activity cliff, and the
metric is again the coefficient of determination. Each pair contributes two rows, with
the difference between the descriptor vectors taken in both orders. The arbitrary
choice of which member comes first therefore cannot influence what the model learns.
The construction also makes the failure mode explicit, because a pair whose two members
carry identical descriptor vectors yields a row of zeros, and any model must then
predict a difference of zero. Framing~B is the primary comparison of this work. The
question being asked is whether a descriptor set can distinguish two molecules that a
fingerprint declares identical, and that distinction is the signed difference within a
pair and nothing else.

In framing~C the unit is again the pair, but the target is categorical. It is whether
the pair is a cliff or a matched control, and the metric is the area under the receiver
operating characteristic curve. The features of a pair have to be invariant to the
order of its two members here, as the label is, and the antisymmetric rows of framing~B
would leave it ambiguous. A pair was therefore described by the elementwise sum and the
elementwise absolute difference concatenated. The two classes are close to balanced in
the development split, at 77 cliff pairs against 68 controls, so the metric is not
distorted by a rare positive. A pair is indivisible in both of the pair framings. Its
rows were therefore assigned to the same fold and resampled together, and the pair is
also the unit of the intervals of Section~\ref{sec:eval}. Treating its two rows as
independent observations would count every pair twice and would narrow every interval
by a factor that the data does not support.

\subsection{Five descriptor sets of increasing computational
cost}\label{sec:rungs}

We compared five descriptor sets, ordered by the cost of producing them, and we refer
to each as a rung of that ladder. The first needs only the molecular graph. The next
two need a docked pose, taken from one receptor conformation and from an ensemble of
eight. The fourth needs an energy evaluation on that pose, and the fifth a
molecular-dynamics trajectory. The ensemble rung is labelled 2e rather than 3 because
it shares its input with rung~2 and differs only in how many receptor conformations
that input comes from. The ordering is the point of the comparison. The question is not
whether a more expensive representation can be built, but whether it buys anything. We
therefore scored every rung with the same algorithms, on the same folds and in the
three framings of Section~\ref{sec:framings}. A difference between two rungs is then a
difference between representations and not between model families.
Table~\ref{tab:rungs} gives the input each rung requires, what its descriptors capture,
the feature count and the cost. Two of those columns carry a qualification. The feature
counts are the two feature sets of rung~1 and, for rung~4, the full set beside the set
that survives the reliability filter. The costs are median measured values, given as
processor time for the first three rungs and as wall-clock time on one GPU for the
simulation. These are not the same unit and are not comparable beyond their order of
magnitude. The number of features is not matched across the rungs either, and that is
deliberate. Each rung was scored in the form in which it would actually be used, and a
comparison that first cut a fingerprint down to 34 features would describe nothing that
anyone does. We also scored a matched-dimensionality arm as a robustness check, by
performing the feature selection inside each training fold so that the held-out fold
can never influence which features are chosen.

Rung~1 uses no three-dimensional information, and it is computed on the standardised
neutral structures. Meanwhile, every structure-based rung uses the protonated form of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. The ladder therefore does not describe one chemical species
throughout, so we scored rung~1 on both forms. The protonated form is never the weaker
of the two. It gains $0.018$ on \framingA{} and $0.010$ on \framingC{} and leaves
\framingB{} unchanged, so the baseline reported throughout is if anything understated.
Two feature sets were carried here. The first is the binary ECFP4 that defined the
cliffs in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. The second is a count-based ECFP4 of the same
width combined with the 217 physicochemical descriptors of RDKit, and we took the
better of the two in every framing. Those 217 are the standard RDKit set, covering
molecular size, polarity, flexibility and a range of topological indices, and they
enter the second feature set alone. Neither feature set dominates the other. Scoring
only one would have handicapped the baseline against which every other rung was
measured, in the direction that flatters the more expensive rungs.

We chose the rung-2 descriptors to test one mechanism and not to cast a wide net. That
mechanism is the proposal that two closely related inhibitors are distinguished by how
persistently each of them engages the two ends of the gorge, and by its position along
the axis between them. A fixed panel of fourteen gorge-lining residues carries the
pose-geometry, MM/GBSA and molecular-dynamics descriptors alike, so that all three are
computed on the same positions. These residues are 72, 74, 86, 121, 124, 203, 286,
295, 297, 334, 337, 338, 341 and 447. Each rung-2 descriptor is the median over the
three docking seeds of Section~\ref{sec:dock}. We kept the spread across seeds for
diagnosis but did not use it as a feature. The same descriptors were also computed over
an ensemble of receptor conformations and are reported separately as rung~2e, because a
single receptor conformation cannot accommodate every binding mode. The ensemble is
4EY4, 4EY7, 4M0F, 6CQX, 6CQY, 6O4X, 6O5V and 6WUY, chosen by clustering on the
side-chain positions of the gorge-lining residues after superposition. Selecting
instead on resolution or on date of deposition would most likely have returned several
copies of one conformation. Each descriptor is the median across the eight, and the
five axis descriptors are dropped because they are defined against the single 4EY7
geometry. Table~\ref{tab:sirung2} names every rung-2 descriptor and marks the ones the
ensemble carries.

Rung~3 is single-trajectory, in that the complex, the protein and the ligand were
taken from the same coordinates with no re-minimisation after separation. The internal bonded
terms therefore cancel exactly and only the intermolecular interaction and the
desolvation remain. The protein term, which is identical for every ligand under a rigid
receptor, was computed once. This is not a rigorous binding free energy and we do not
present it as one, because absolute values obtained from a single post-docking snapshot
are not defensible. It is on the ladder because it is what a structure-based study
routinely does at this cost. It was computed on the same pose as rung~2, so the two
differ by the energy evaluation and by nothing else. Its 22 descriptors are the
interaction and desolvation terms of Table~\ref{tab:sirung3}, together with a
per-residue decomposition over the same fourteen residues. Rung~4 is described in
Section~\ref{sec:md}. Of its 32 dynamic descriptors, which Table~\ref{tab:sirung4}
names one by one, 11 survived the reliability filter described there. That filter
removes those whose variation between repeated simulations of one ligand is comparable
to their variation between ligands. The heavy-atom count is carried alongside either
set as a size covariate and not as a dynamic descriptor, so the two feature counts
scored are 33 and 12. We scored both sets, because reporting only one would leave open
whether a result is a property of the descriptors or of the filter. Both the splits of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and the designation of framing~B as the primary comparison
predate the first of these scores by more than a fortnight, so neither was chosen once
the expensive answer was known.

\subsection{Receptor model and docking protocol}\label{sec:dock}

We prepared the receptor model from the X-ray crystal structure of recombinant hAChE in
complex with donepezil, whose PDB ID is 4EY7 \citep{cheung2012} and whose resolution is
\SI{2.35}{\angstrom}. We chose this structure because donepezil is itself a
dual-binding-site inhibitor. Chain~A was retained and the second copy in the asymmetric
unit was discarded. The enzyme is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(A), where
donepezil in its crystal pose marks the active site at the base of a narrow gorge. The
architecture of that gorge is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(B). An aromatic
residue sits at each end, which are Trp86 at the catalytic anionic site (CAS) and
Trp286 at the peripheral anionic site (PAS) near the mouth. Tyr337 sits between the two
and adopts different rotamers in different complexes, and we therefore refer to it as
the gate. The CZ3 atoms of the two indole rings were measured \SI{15.6}{\angstrom}
apart in this complex. Inhibitors designed to span that distance are an established
design principle and not a premise introduced here \citep{grabowska2025}, because the
peripheral anionic site has been reported to accelerate the aggregation of
$\beta$-amyloid alongside the catalytic function of the enzyme. We built the descriptor
sets of Section~\ref{sec:rungs} to test this principle. Preparation of the same
receptor for simulation differs and is described in Section~\ref{sec:md}, because it
becomes relevant only once a force field is applied. The receptor is rigid throughout
the docking.

We docked all ligands into the prepared 4EY7 receptor by using AutoDock Vina~1.2.7
\citep{vina_orig,vina}. The search box was a cube of \SI{30}{\angstrom} per side
centred at $(-14.257, -42.544, 30.646)$~\AA{}, which is midway between the CAS and the
PAS so that poses spanning the gorge were accessible. We set the exhaustiveness to 32.
Ligand structures were generated from the protonated SMILES of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} by adding hydrogens, embedding a single conformer with the
ETKDGv3 method under a fixed random seed and optimising it with the MMFF94 force field,
all by using RDKit~2026.03.1 \citep{rdkit}. The resulting structures were converted to
the PDBQT format by using Meeko~0.8 \citep{meeko}. Each ligand was docked with three
independent seeds, because the search of Vina is stochastic and a single run can settle
on a different pose. The cationic state matters here, as the catalytic site binds its
ligands through a cation--$\pi$ interaction with Trp86. The pose-geometry descriptors
were computed on the top-ranked pose of each seed and reduced to their median, and the
spread across seeds was kept so that unstable poses could be identified. Every rung
above the first begins from these poses. The MM/GBSA evaluation of rung~3 uses the same
coordinates, and each simulated system is built with its ligand in its docked pose. The
docking validation therefore underwrites the whole structure-based half of the ladder
and not rung~2 alone.

\subsection{Molecular dynamics simulations and descriptor extraction}\label{sec:md}

The simulations used the same 4EY7 coordinates as the docking, prepared differently.
The crystal structure does not resolve residues 259 to 264 and 495 to 497. The
force-field setup treated each chain as continuous and bridged both gaps with peptide
bonds of 7.11 and \SI{4.05}{\angstrom}, against an equilibrium length of
\SI{1.33}{\angstrom}. Those two bonds carried 166{,}885 of the system's
185{,}963~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole} of bond energy, and a minimisation would have
pulled the flanking segments together and deformed the fold. We attempted to rebuild
the loops and abandoned it, because the best of four attempts gave four non-proline cis
peptide bonds in one loop of six residues, against a natural rate near 0.03 per cent.
What had to go was the fabricated bond and not the absence of the loop. We therefore
divided the chain at the breaks into the segments 4--258, 265--494 and 498--541, gave
each its own chain identifier, and capped it with an acetyl group at its N-terminal and
an N-methylamide group at its C-terminal side. We used neutral caps, because charged
termini would have added six artificial formal charges on the surface. The caps were
placed by internal coordinates, so that the procedure is deterministic. All six caps
sit on surface loops. The nearest cap atom lies \SI{15.5}{\angstrom} from the closest
gorge-lining heavy atom and \SI{28.9}{\angstrom} from Trp86 at the gorge floor. Bond
energy fell to 1{,}145~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole} and the maximum force from
$3.3\times10^{8}$ to $2.1\times10^{5}$~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole\per\nano\metre}. No
atom that the two receptors share moved, the gorge-lining side chains and all 529
shared C$\alpha$ alike, and their displacement is zero at the \SI{0.001}{\angstrom}
precision the coordinate file holds. The docked poses therefore remain valid and
nothing earlier in this work had to be repeated.

We built one system per ligand and not per trajectory, so that a replicate differs from
its parent only in the velocity seed and tests the sampling and not the structure. The
protein was described by the amber14SB force field \citep{amber_ff14sb}, the ligand by
OpenFF Sage 2.3.0 \citep{openff_sage} through a SMIRNOFF template generator, and the
solvent by the TIP3P water model \citep{tip3p} with sodium chloride at
\SI{0.15}{\mole\per\litre}. This is the combination we used for the MM/GBSA calculation
of Section~\ref{sec:rungs}, because a difference there would have turned a comparison
of rungs into a comparison of force fields. Each ligand was placed in its docked pose
in a cubic box of \SI{100.1}{\angstrom} per side, giving between 96{,}220 and
96{,}298 atoms. Table~\ref{tab:system} reports the composition, where a range is the
minimum and the maximum across systems and a single value means every system shares it.
Two quantities were checked for every system instead of being assumed. The first is the
net charge after counterions, which is at most $2.8\times10^{-13}\,e$ across all 217
development systems. The second is the closest receptor--ligand approach at build
time. Heavy atom against heavy atom this runs from 2.38 to \SI{3.52}{\angstrom} with no
clash in any system, and the 1.67 to \SI{2.66}{\angstrom} that heavy atoms and
hydrogens reach is the geometry of a hydrogen bond rather than of an overlap.

Each system was energy-minimised by steepest descent until the maximum force fell below
\SI{100}{\kilo\joule\per\mole\per\nano\metre}. It was then equilibrated for
\SI{100}{\pico\second} in the canonical ensemble with the solute position-restrained,
and for a further \SI{100}{\pico\second} in the isothermal-isobaric ensemble without
restraints. Production used a \SI{2}{\femto\second} time step with the bonds to
hydrogen constrained by LINCS, a Verlet neighbour list and particle-mesh Ewald
electrostatics. The cutoff was \SI{1.0}{\nano\metre} for both the real-space Coulomb
and the van der Waals interactions, with a long-range dispersion correction applied to
the energy and the pressure. Temperature was held by the velocity-rescaling thermostat
at \SI{300}{\kelvin} with a coupling time of \SI{0.1}{\pico\second}. Pressure was held
by the c-rescale barostat at \SI{1}{\bar}, isotropic, with a coupling time of
\SI{2}{\pico\second} and a compressibility of $4.5\times10^{-5}$~\si{\per\bar}.
Coordinates were written to disk every \SI{10}{\pico\second}. We ran the simulations by
using the GPU-resident mdrun of GROMACS~2023 \citep{gromacs}, which keeps the whole
step on the accelerator. We completed 283 production runs over the 271
ligands, amounting to 8{,}319~ns. The 217 development ligands account for 229 of these
runs, of which 211 were simulated once and 6 three times for the replicate pilot, and
each of the 54 test ligands carries one run. Production was \SI{25}{\nano\second} per ligand. The first
\SI{5}{\nano\second} was discarded and the \SI{20}{\nano\second} that follows supplied
every descriptor, so every ligand carries the same record. Fifty-six runs of the first
batch were continued past \SI{25}{\nano\second}, 39 of them to \SI{50}{\nano\second} and
the rest to between 34 and \SI{45}{\nano\second}. They enter this work only as the
evidence that \SI{20}{\nano\second} of analysed trajectory is not too short, and 55 of
them run long enough to supply a \SI{30}{\nano\second} window.

Thirty-two descriptors were computed from each trajectory, falling into the three
design classes of Figure~\ref{fig:mdflow}. Persistence quantities measure the fraction
of the analysed window over which a contact or a position is held, fluctuation
quantities the amplitude of motion, and event counts the rate at which something
happens. None is a frame-averaged rung-2 quantity, because averaging a pose descriptor
over a trajectory would test the averaging rather than the dynamics. The fluctuation
class rests on the root-mean-square fluctuation of an atom about its own mean position
\citep{mdfluct,rmsf_bfactor}, taken over the ligand atoms and the gorge-lining side
chains. The Tyr337 side chain is followed separately, because its rotation gates the
gorge \citep{gating1998,gorge2017}. It contributes the spread of its $\chi_1$ dihedral
as a fluctuation quantity and the crossings between rotamer wells as an event count.
One further quantity counts the waters hydrogen bonding to ligand and protein at once
\citep{water_ladbury,water_levy}. All are computed on the same fourteen gorge-lining
residues, with Trp86 at the CAS, Trp286 at the PAS and Tyr337 as the gate, and the
heavy-atom count is carried beside them as a size covariate rather than as one of the
32. Before anything is measured, each molecule is made whole along its bonds, prevented
from jumping between consecutive frames and placed in the periodic image of the first
frame. Only then is the receptor superposed by the Kabsch algorithm, all of it by using
MDAnalysis~2.10.0 \citep{mdanalysis}. An earlier treatment that compared coordinates
against a frozen reference changed 19 of the 32 descriptors. Which of them can carry a
pair endpoint was decided by a replicate pilot, under a rule fixed before any replicate
had been scored. A descriptor qualifies only when the spread between ligands is at
least twice the spread between replicates of the same ligand. This is the reliability
$\sigma^2_{\mathrm{lig}}/(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{lig}} + 2\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rep}})$ written
as a ratio of standard deviations. The factor of two is a judgement made blind, and a
difference between two ligands carries the replicate noise twice over. The replicate
spread rests on six ligands simulated three times each, which is a narrow basis stated
here as such, while the spread between ligands is taken over all 217. Of the 160
combinations of descriptor and analysis window tested, 100 are dominated by the

\subsection{Preprocessing and learning algorithms}\label{sec:algo}

Every preprocessing step was a stage of a pipeline rather than a transformation applied
to the matrix beforehand. Each was therefore fitted on the training part of a fold and
then applied unchanged to the held-out part. The structure-based rungs began with a
median imputation, because a pose or a trajectory occasionally failed to supply what a
descriptor needs. We took that median from the training fold alone, because a
replacement computed on the whole matrix would carry information from the data it is
then asked to predict. A zero-variance filter followed, which removes the fingerprint
bits and the per-residue counts that were constant across the ligands of a fold. We
then standardised the features for the linear, latent-variable, kernel and
distance-based learners, whose behaviour depends on the relative scale of the features.
We left the two tree ensembles unscaled, because they are invariant to any monotone
rescaling. Rung~1 needed no imputation stage, because the non-finite values that RDKit
returns for a handful of its topological descriptors were set to zero when the matrix
was built.

Six regression families carried framings~A and~B. We chose them to span the model
classes rather than to find the best one. They are ridge regression \citep{ridge},
partial least squares \citep{pls}, $k$-nearest neighbours \citep{knn}, a support vector
machine with a radial basis function kernel \citep{svm}, a random forest \citep{rf} and
histogram-based gradient boosting \citep{gbm}, all as implemented in
scikit-learn~1.9.0 \citep{sklearn}. A linear model, a latent-variable model, an
instance-based one, a kernel method and two ensembles between them cover the shapes a
structure-activity relationship is usually assumed to take. A rung that fails on all
six has therefore not simply been given the wrong model. We fixed the hyperparameters
before any rung was scored and used the same values at every rung, which
Table~\ref{tab:algorithms} gives beside the estimator each family uses. We ran no inner
search over them, because tuning each rung separately would let a comparison between
representations absorb a difference in how hard each one had been tuned.

Framing~C carried five classification families. It is one fewer than the regression
suite because partial least squares has no classification counterpart, so it is missing
there rather than having been tried and failed. Every rung met the same families in the
same framings, which is what allows a difference between two rungs to be read as a
difference between their descriptors. A family appearing in both suites kept its
hyperparameters. The regression and the classification results therefore differ by the
task and by the metric and not by the configuration of the learner.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports the best value a rung reached over its descriptor arms
and its families, and Tables~\ref{tab:benchreg} and~\ref{tab:benchclf} hold the full
grid behind it.

We scored a matched-dimensionality arm alongside the main comparison, in which the
wider feature set is cut to the width of the narrower one before the learner sees it.
The selector is a univariate filter on the $F$ statistic, of the regression or the
classification form according to the framing, and we placed it inside the pipeline with
everything else. That placement is the point of the arm rather than a detail of its
implementation. Choosing the $k$ features on the whole matrix and cross-validating
afterwards lets the held-out fold influence which features it is then asked to predict
from. The inflation that follows is substantial and it is also invisible, because such
an arm still reports a cross-validated score that looks honest. Holding the selection
inside the fold cost nothing beyond the time to refit it five times.

\subsection{Model evaluation and statistical analysis}\label{sec:eval}

Every model was scored by five-fold cross-validation on the folds frozen in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. We pooled the out-of-fold predictions before computing any
metric, so that a fold which happens to be easy cannot offset one which is hard. The
number reported for a rung is the best it reached over its descriptor arms and its
algorithm families, and the same selection is made on both sides of every comparison.
The two regression framings were judged primarily by the coefficient of determination,
with the root mean squared error, the mean absolute error and the Spearman rank
correlation recorded beside it. We keep the rank correlation because a descriptor set
can order the differences within pairs correctly while misjudging their size, and the
two metrics together separate that case from a set which does neither. We also recorded
the observed and predicted means over the cliff pairs and the control pairs separately,
because a model can otherwise reach a respectable coefficient of determination on the
pooled pairs by predicting the controls well and the cliffs not at all. Framing~C was
judged by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, which suits a
design of 77 cliff pairs against 68 controls. Average precision, the Matthews
correlation coefficient and balanced accuracy were recorded alongside it, the last two
at a probability of one half. Every number in this work other than those of
Section~\ref{sec:testsplit} is a development-split estimate. The sealed test split was
opened once, after all of them had been computed, and Section~\ref{sec:testsplit}
reports that single pass.

Comparisons of a descriptor between cliff pairs and control pairs were made with the
two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test, because those differences are bounded below by zero
and strongly skewed. Monotone association was measured by the Spearman rank
correlation, and the Pearson correlation appears only where a linear relation is itself
the claim. Any comparison of this kind is confounded by molecular size, because the two
members of a cliff pair differ by 4.4 heavy atoms on average against 1.8 for a control
pair. A descriptor that tracks size then separates the groups for a reason unrelated to
potency. We therefore repeated each test within bins of equal heavy-atom difference and
again on the residual after regressing the descriptor on that difference, and we
treated a descriptor as surviving only where it held in both. These tests are
descriptive and carry no correction for multiplicity, because the inferential weight of
this work rests on the primary endpoint fixed in advance and on the interval around it.

An interval on each comparison came from a bootstrap over the units of its framing,
with 300 replicates. The unit is the ligand in framing~A and the pair in framings~B
and~C, and a pair was resampled whole, so its two antisymmetric rows travel together.
Inside every replicate we re-selected the best over the descriptor arms and the
algorithm families on both sides, because choosing the best is part of the procedure
whose uncertainty is being measured. Rung~1 entered with both of its feature sets for
the same reason, because the winner between them depends on the framing. We took the
interval as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled gap. It carries a bias
that we state rather than correct. Running folds fixed beforehand over a resample puts
duplicated units on both sides of a fold boundary and inflates the absolute scores.
That inflation applies identically to the two sides, so the gap is far less affected
than either level. A bootstrap median is accordingly not a performance estimate and
must not be quoted as one. The resolution of the comparison was measured rather than
assumed, by pushing synthetic descriptor matrices through the same support vector
machine and the same folds, with the rung-1 baseline re-simulated in every replicate so
that its own uncertainty is carried. A gain of 0.26 in the coefficient of determination
is detected in 90 per cent of simulations and a gain of 0.11 in 36 per cent, with a
mean interval width of 0.32, which is the resolution this design can claim.
