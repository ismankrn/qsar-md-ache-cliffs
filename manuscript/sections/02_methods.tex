\section{Methods}

In this section, we introduce the procedures to construct and to evaluate the
descriptor sets compared in this work. We first describe the construction of the
activity-cliff dataset together with the splits on which everything was scored, the
three framings in which every descriptor set was scored, and the four descriptor sets
themselves. We then describe the two procedures that produce the more expensive of those sets,
namely the receptor model with its docking and validation, and the molecular dynamics
simulations. The descriptors derived from the trajectories follow, together with the
periodic-boundary treatment they require and the replicate pilot that filters them.
The model suite, the interval estimates and the power calculation close the section.

\subsection{Dataset, activity-cliff definition and splits}\label{sec:dataset}

The dataset was built in three steps, in which the activity data were curated, the
activity cliffs were defined, and the pairs were assembled and then divided into a
development and a sealed test part. The criteria by which we chose the target itself
are stated after the first two steps, because both were needed in order to apply them. Activity data for human
acetylcholinesterase (hAChE), whose ChEMBL target identifier is CHEMBL220, were then
retrieved from ChEMBL. A record was kept only when it came from a binding assay,
reported IC\textsubscript{50}, $K_\mathrm{i}$ or $K_\mathrm{d}$ with an exact relation
in nanomolar units, carried a pChEMBL value, was free of ChEMBL's own data-validity
flags and was not marked a potential duplicate. A single assay type was then kept, the most
frequent one, which is IC\textsubscript{50} here. Mixing assay types is unsafe,
as a pair formed from an IC\textsubscript{50} and a $K_\mathrm{i}$ measurement can
satisfy a cliff criterion by virtue of the assay alone. Structures were standardised
by stripping salts, keeping the largest covalent fragment and neutralising formal
charges from the deposited representation, and those below six heavy atoms were
removed. Replicate measurements of one standardised structure were collapsed to their median,
and a structure whose replicates disagreed by more than one log unit in standard
deviation was discarded instead of being averaged. A compound measured inconsistently
can otherwise enter a cliff pair on the strength of its own measurement error. The curated set holds 5{,}609 compounds spanning pChEMBL
4.00 to 10.96.

Similarity was computed as the Tanimoto coefficient on binary ECFP4, i.e. Morgan
fingerprints of radius 2 folded to 2{,}048 bits. A pair was treated as near-identical
at a coefficient of at least 0.90, and as an activity cliff when its two members also
differed by at least 2.0 log units in pChEMBL, a hundredfold difference in potency. The pairs were further divided
according to whether their two members differ in
constitution or only in stereochemistry, comparing their canonical SMILES with the stereochemistry stripped, and only the
constitutional pairs were treated as informative.
ECFP is blind to stereochemistry, so a stereo-blind fingerprint cannot resolve a
stereoisomer pair at all, while any method that uses three-dimensional coordinates
resolves it trivially. Within the curated hAChE set, 421 pairs cleared the similarity
criterion with a difference of at least one log unit, 110 of them reached the
hundredfold criterion, and 100 of those were constitutional.

We took hAChE from six proteins examined against three entry criteria, namely at least
80 compounds surviving the curation above, a potency range of at least 3 log units,
and at least 25 constitutional activity cliffs. The third criterion rests entirely on
the cliff definition above, and the same thresholds were applied to all six targets,
so the definition was fixed before any target was chosen. All six satisfied the first
two criteria, and two of them satisfied the third. We chose hAChE from those two for two reasons, the first being that its constitutional
pairs dominate most strongly, at 100 against 10 stereochemical ones. The second is
that its binding site gives a mechanistic reason to expect geometric cliffs, since its
two recognition sites sit at opposite ends of a narrow gorge and its inhibitors are
designed to span them, as described in Section~\ref{sec:dock}. The enzyme is also a
therapeutic target in Alzheimer's disease, and Section~\ref{sec:composition} reports
the counts for all six proteins. We examined these six and did not search ChEMBL
systematically, so any comparison between targets holds among them and not in general.

The compound pool was then filtered for simulation feasibility, and a compound was
kept only when it met five conditions. It had to be composed of the elements H, C, N, O, F, S, Cl, Br, I and P, and to carry
no radical electrons. It also had to have a molecular weight between 150 and 700~Da,
at most 55 heavy atoms and at most 18 rotatable bonds, and to be embeddable in three
dimensions by ETKDGv3. This removed 239 of the 5{,}609
compounds, most of them on molecular weight or on rotatable-bond count. We applied the
filter to the whole pool before any pairing, because a pair is lost as soon as either
of its members is removed, so that filtering first keeps every surviving pair intact
by construction. Of the 100 constitutional
cliff pairs, 97 survived with both members intact, formed from 99 distinct compounds.

A control pair was near-identical under the same floor of 0.90, but its two members
differed by at most 0.30 log units, less than twofold. The two groups then differ in
the property under test and in as little else as possible. Stereoisomer pairs were excluded here on
the same grounds as above, and the controls were drawn to reproduce the
joint distribution of similarity and mean potency of the cliff pairs over four
similarity and five potency bins, using each control compound once. The procedure
yielded 86 control pairs from 172 compounds, and the matching is close on both
quantities. The median Tanimoto coefficient is 1.000 for the cliff pairs against 0.993
for the controls, and the mean pair-mean potency 6.64 against 6.58, while the quantity
under test differs by construction at 2.66 log units against 0.13. The final set holds
271 ligands and 183 pairs, 97 cliff and 86 control. Each ligand was prepared and simulated once, while a pair refers
to two ligands, as a compound may belong to more than one pair. Each ligand was prepared in two forms, of which the standardised neutral structure
carries the fingerprints and the pair definition above. The dominant protonation state
at pH 7.4, assigned by explicit rules, is the one that enters the docking and the
simulations. Every ligand
carries a net positive charge in that second form, 170 at $+1$ and 101 at $+2$.

This set was then divided once into a development and a test part, with the random
seed being 20260902, and was never revised afterwards. The unit of assignment is not the ligand but the connected component of the graph
whose vertices are the ligands and whose edges are the pairs. Every ligand belongs to
at least one pair, and a split that separated a pair would let a model memorise the
shared scaffold from the training side, so the 271 ligands were handled as the 110
components that they form. These were stratified by whether they contain a cliff pair
and by their mean potency, and 20 per cent of each stratum was assigned to the test
split. The remainder were distributed over five cross-validation folds balanced
jointly on cliff pairs and on ligand count, since either quantity alone leaves the
other badly skewed. Table~\ref{tab:splits}
gives the result, and no pair has its two members on opposite sides of the test
boundary or of any fold boundary, which was verified on the written splits. The test
split was to be opened only once a single descriptor set and algorithm had been
selected, which did not happen, so no model reported in this work has been scored on
it.

\subsection{Three framings of the prediction problem}\label{sec:framings}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{\textbf{The three framings.} The unit of each, the quantity it predicts,
    the features of one row, the metric, and the number of algorithm families.}
  \label{tab:framings}
  \input{../results/31_framings.tex}
\end{table}

A comparison between descriptor sets has no single answer, as what counts as an
improvement depends on what is being predicted. Activity cliffs are the standard
counter-example to QSAR for exactly this reason, since models that predict potency
well across a series are reported to fail on the near-identical pairs within it
\citep{vantilborg2022, dablander2023}. The three framings below are different
functions of the same descriptors, so a result on one does not determine a result on
another, and separating cliff pairs from control pairs does not require predicting the
size of the difference within a pair. Three framings were therefore scored for every
descriptor set, on the folds of Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and with the same
algorithms, so that the three results differ in the question being asked and in
nothing else. Table~\ref{tab:framings} sets them side by side, with $\mathbf{d}_a$ and
$\mathbf{d}_b$ being the descriptor vectors of the two ligands of a pair and the
number in parentheses the count of units available in the development split, and the
three are taken in turn below.

In framing~A the unit is the ligand, the target is its pChEMBL value, and the metric
is the coefficient of determination over the out-of-fold predictions. The predictions of the five folds are pooled before the metric is computed, so a fold
that happens to be easy cannot be averaged against one that is hard. This is ordinary
potency prediction, the task on which the QSAR literature is calibrated, and we
included it for two reasons. A descriptor set that cannot predict potency at all is no
candidate for predicting a difference in potency. A result on this task can also be
placed beside published models in a way that a pairwise result cannot.

In framing~B the unit is the pair and the target is the signed difference in pChEMBL
between its two members, scored again by the coefficient of determination, which is
the quantity that defines an activity cliff. Each pair contributes two rows, with the
difference between the descriptor vectors taken in both orders, so that the arbitrary
choice of which member comes first cannot influence what the model learns. The construction also makes the failure mode explicit, since a pair whose two members
carry identical descriptor vectors yields a row of zeros and any model must then
predict a difference of zero. Framing~B is the primary comparison of this work, as the
question being asked is whether a descriptor set can distinguish two molecules that a
fingerprint declares identical. That distinction is the signed difference within a
pair and nothing else.

In framing~C the unit is again the pair, but the target is categorical, namely whether
the pair is a cliff or a matched control, and the metric is the area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve. The features of a pair have to be invariant
to the order of its two members here, as the label is, and the antisymmetric rows of
framing~B would leave it ambiguous. A pair was therefore described by the elementwise
sum and the elementwise absolute difference concatenated. This framing uses five algorithm families, not six, as partial least squares
regression has no classification counterpart. The two classes are close to balanced in
the development split, at 77 cliff pairs against 68 controls, so the metric is not
distorted by a rare positive. A pair is indivisible in both of the pair framings, so
its rows were assigned to the same fold and resampled together, and the pair is also
the unit of the intervals of Section~\ref{sec:eval}. Treating its two rows as
independent observations would count every pair twice and would narrow every interval
by a factor that the data does not support.

\subsection{Four descriptor sets of increasing computational
cost}\label{sec:rungs}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{\textbf{The four descriptor sets.} The input each rung requires, what its
    descriptors capture, the number of features it was scored with, and the cost of
    producing it for one ligand.}
  \label{tab:rungs}
  \input{../results/32_rungs.tex}
\end{table}

We compared four descriptor families, ordered by the cost of producing them, and we
refer to each below as a rung of that ladder. The first needs only the molecular
graph, the second a docked pose, the third an energy evaluation on that pose and the
fourth a molecular-dynamics trajectory. The ordering is the point of the comparison,
as the question is not whether a more expensive representation can be built but
whether it buys anything. We therefore scored every rung with the same algorithms, on
the same folds and in the three framings of Section~\ref{sec:framings}, so that a
difference between two rungs is a difference between representations and not between
model families. Two columns of Table~\ref{tab:rungs} carry a qualification, the first being that the
feature counts are the two feature sets of rung~1 and, for rung~4, the full set beside
the set that survives the reliability filter. The costs are median measured values, given as
processor time for the first three rungs and as wall-clock time on one GPU for the
simulation, which are not the same unit and are not comparable beyond their order of
magnitude.

Rung~1 uses no three-dimensional information, and it is computed on the standardised
neutral structures, while every structure-based rung uses the protonated form of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. The ladder therefore does not describe one
chemical species throughout, so we rescored rung~1 on the protonated structures as
well. It does not fall, gaining 0.018 on \framingA{} and 0.010 on \framingC{} and
changing by less than $10^{-6}$ on \framingB{}, so the published baseline is if
anything slightly understated. Two feature sets were carried here, the
binary ECFP4 that defined the cliffs in Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and a count-based
ECFP4 of the same width combined with the 217 physicochemical descriptors of RDKit,
and we took the better of the two in every framing. Neither dominates the other, and
scoring only one would have handicapped the baseline against which every other rung
was measured, in the direction that flatters the more expensive rungs.

We chose the rung-2 descriptors to test one mechanism and not to cast a wide net. That
mechanism is the proposal that two closely related inhibitors are distinguished by how
persistently each of them engages the two ends of the gorge, and by its position along
the axis between them. A fixed panel of fourteen gorge-lining residues, i.e. residues 72, 74, 86, 121,
124, 203, 286, 295, 297, 334, 337, 338, 341 and 447, is used from here on, so that the
pose-geometry, MM/GBSA and molecular-dynamics descriptors are computed on the same
positions. Each rung-2 descriptor is the median over the three docking seeds of
Section~\ref{sec:dock}, and we kept the spread across seeds for diagnosis but did not
use it as a feature. The fourteen residues of that panel are the same ones on which
the MM/GBSA decomposition and the trajectory descriptors are computed, so the three
structure-based rungs describe the same positions.

The same descriptors were also computed over an ensemble of receptor conformations
and are reported separately as rung~2e. What limits the docking is the rigidity of a single
receptor and not the protocol, which is established in Section~\ref{sec:dock}. We
selected eight structures, namely 4EY4, 4EY7, 4M0F, 6CQX, 6CQY, 6O4X, 6O5V and 6WUY,
by clustering on the side-chain positions of the gorge-lining residues after
superposition. Selecting on resolution or on date of deposition would most likely have
returned several copies of one conformation. Each descriptor is the median across the
eight, and the five axis descriptors are dropped, being defined against the single
4EY7 geometry.

Rung~3 is single-trajectory, in that the complex, the protein and the ligand were
taken from the same coordinates with no re-minimisation after separation. The internal
bonded terms therefore cancel exactly and only the intermolecular interaction and the
desolvation remain, while the protein term, which is identical for every ligand under
a rigid receptor, was computed once. This is not a rigorous binding free energy and we
do not present it as one, as absolute values obtained from a single post-docking
snapshot are not defensible. It is on the ladder because it is what a structure-based study routinely does at this
cost, and the question is whether that routine step buys anything. It was computed on
the same pose as rung~2, so the two differ by the energy evaluation and by nothing
else.

Rung~4 is described in Sections~\ref{sec:md} and \ref{sec:mddesc}, where the
simulations and their descriptors are set out. Both the splits of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and the designation of framing~B as the primary comparison
predate the first of its scores by more than a fortnight, which the version history of
the analysis code records, so neither was chosen once the expensive answer was known. Twelve of its 33
descriptors survived the reliability filter of Section~\ref{sec:filter}, which removes
those whose variation between repeated simulations of one ligand is comparable to
their variation between ligands. Both sets were scored, as reporting only one would
leave open whether a result is a property of the descriptors or of the filter. The
number of features is not matched across the rungs, and that is deliberate. Each was
scored in the form in which it would actually be used, and a comparison that first cut
a fingerprint down to 34 features would describe nothing that anyone does. A
difference between two rungs is therefore a difference between representations at
their natural dimensionality. A matched-dimensionality arm was also scored as a robustness
check, with the feature selection performed inside each training fold so that
the held-out fold can never influence which features are chosen.

\subsection{Receptor model, docking protocol and its
validation}\label{sec:dock}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figure1_hAChE_structure.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Human acetylcholinesterase and its catalytic gorge (PDB 4EY7).}
    (A)~Overall fold, rainbow-coloured from N- to C-terminus; donepezil in the
    crystal pose marks the gorge as black spheres. (B)~The architecture of the gorge, formed by Trp86 at
    the catalytic anionic site, Trp286 at the peripheral anionic site, the swinging
    Tyr337 gate between them, and the Ser203--His447--Glu334 catalytic triad.}
  \label{fig:structure}
\end{figure}

We prepared the receptor model from the X-ray crystal structure of recombinant hAChE
in complex with donepezil, with the PDB ID being 4EY7 \citep{cheung2012} and the
resolution being \SI{2.35}{\angstrom}. Chain~A was retained and the second copy in the
asymmetric unit was discarded. The enzyme is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(A), where donepezil in its crystal
pose marks the active site at the base of a narrow gorge. The architecture of that
gorge is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(B), with an aromatic residue at each end,
namely Trp86 at the catalytic anionic site (CAS) and Trp286 at the peripheral anionic
site (PAS) near the mouth. Tyr337 sits between the two, adopts different rotamers in
different complexes, and is therefore referred to as the gate. In this complex the CZ3 atoms of the two indole rings were
measured \SI{15.6}{\angstrom} apart, and donepezil spans that distance while
contacting both residues.

Donepezil is itself a dual-binding-site inhibitor, which is why this structure was
chosen. Inhibitors designed to span both ends of the gorge are an established design
principle and not a premise introduced here \citep{grabowska2025}. The peripheral
anionic site has been reported to accelerate the aggregation of $\beta$-amyloid
alongside the catalytic function of the enzyme, and it is this principle that we built
the descriptor sets of Section~\ref{sec:rungs} to test. Preparation of the same receptor for simulation differs and is described in
Section~\ref{sec:md}, since it becomes relevant only once a force field is applied.
The receptor is rigid throughout the docking, and the consequences of that are
measured below.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figure2_redocking_validation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Redocking validation, and the limit of a rigid receptor.}
    (A)~Donepezil redocked into its own structure reproduces the crystal pose to
    0.75~\AA{} RMSD over five seeds. (B)~Huperzine~A docked into its own receptor
    (4EY5) succeeds. (C)~The same ligand cross-docked into 4EY7 fails at
    3.10~\AA{}, because the Tyr337 rotamer of this structure occludes the true
    pose.}
  \label{fig:redock}
\end{figure}

We docked all ligands into the prepared 4EY7 receptor by using AutoDock Vina
\citep{vina}. The search box was a cube of \SI{30}{\angstrom} per side centred at
$(-14.257, -42.544, 30.646)$~\AA{}, midway between the CAS and the PAS so that poses
spanning the gorge were accessible, and we set the exhaustiveness to 32. Ligand structures were generated from the protonated SMILES of
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} by adding hydrogens, embedding a single conformer with the
ETKDGv3 method under a fixed random seed and optimising it with the MMFF94 force
field, all by using RDKit \citep{rdkit}. The resulting structures were converted to
the PDBQT format by using Meeko \citep{meeko}, and each ligand was docked with three
independent seeds. The cationic state matters here, as the catalytic site binds its
ligands through a cation--$\pi$ interaction with Trp86. The
pose-geometry descriptors were computed on the top-ranked pose of each seed and
reduced to their median, and the spread across seeds was kept so that unstable poses
could be identified.

We validated the protocol by redocking four ligands whose complexes with hAChE have
been solved, with five seeds per ligand and a pose counted as successful below
\SI{2.0}{\angstrom} RMSD from the crystal pose. Donepezil was reproduced at
$0.745 \pm 0.009$~\AA{} in five of five runs, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:redock}(A), and territrem~B at $0.740 \pm 0.012$~\AA{} in five of
five. Galantamine gave $2.463 \pm 1.709$~\AA{} and succeeded in four of five, and
huperzine~A gave $3.098 \pm 0.011$~\AA{} and failed in all five. That failure was systematic and not
stochastic, as the spread across seeds was \SI{0.011}{\angstrom}, and we tested two
explanations. Repeating the docking with
boxes of 18, 22, 26 and \SI{30}{\angstrom} per side left the RMSD of huperzine~A
between 3.05 and \SI{3.18}{\angstrom}, so box size was not the cause. Docking each ligand into the structure of its own complex instead succeeded, with
huperzine~A at \SI{0.23}{\angstrom} in 4EY5, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:redock}(B),
and galantamine at \SI{0.81}{\angstrom} in 4EY6. The same ligand and the same protocol
failed in 4EY7, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:redock}(C), so that the two panels differ
only in the receptor. The Tyr337 side chain differs by \SI{2.53}{\angstrom} between 4EY5 and
4EY7 and occludes the true huperzine~A pose in the latter. The limitation is therefore the rigidity of the
receptor and not the docking protocol, which is the reason for the ensemble docking of
Section~\ref{sec:rungs} and, ultimately, for the molecular dynamics of
Section~\ref{sec:md}. Vina also proved strongly stochastic for small ligands, since galantamine gave 5.88
and \SI{1.60}{\angstrom} on different seeds under otherwise identical settings, which
is why we fixed the seeds and ran three replicates for every ligand.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_interactions.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The reference binding mode, and docking checked at the level of

    where the colour of each edge gives the type of interaction. (b)~The same

The pose-geometry and molecular-dynamics descriptors are defined in terms of residue
contacts and not of RMSD, so we validated the protocol a second time at the level of
interactions. Interaction fingerprints were computed for the crystal
pose and the redocked pose of donepezil by using ProLIF \citep{prolif2021}, with bond orders
transferred from the docked structure so that the two poses carry identical chemistry
and differ only in their coordinates. The crystal binding mode is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:interactions}(a) and the comparison between the two fingerprints in
Figure~\ref{fig:interactions}(b). Six residues are contacted in both poses, five in the crystal pose only and two in the
docked pose only. Every typed interaction at the three named sites is recovered,
namely the hydrophobic and $\pi$-stacking contacts at Trp86 and at Trp286 and the
cation--$\pi$ and hydrophobic contacts at Tyr337, while what is lost on docking is van
der Waals contact alone with Ser203 and His447. We used the typing for validation only, and it enters no descriptor set.

\subsection{Molecular dynamics}\label{sec:md}

The receptor used for simulation is not the one used for docking. The crystal
structure does not resolve residues 259 to 264 and 495 to 497, and the force-field
setup treated each chain as continuous and bridged both gaps with peptide bonds of
7.11 and \SI{4.05}{\angstrom}. Those two bonds carried 166{,}885 of the system's
185{,}963~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole} of bond energy, and a minimisation would have
pulled the flanking segments together and deformed the fold. Rebuilding the loops was attempted and abandoned, as the best of four attempts gave four non-proline cis
peptide bonds in one loop of six residues against a natural rate near 0.03 per cent.
What had to go was the fabricated bond and not the absence of the loop. We therefore
divided the chain at the breaks into the segments 4--258, 265--494 and 498--541, gave
each its own chain identifier, and capped it with an acetyl group at its N-terminal
and an N-methylamide group at its C-terminal side. We used neutral caps, as charged
termini would have added six artificial formal charges on the surface, and placed them
by internal coordinates, so that the procedure is deterministic. Bond energy fell to 1{,}145~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole} and the maximum force from
$3.3\times10^{8}$ to $2.1\times10^{5}$~\si{\kilo\joule\per\mole\per\nano\metre}. The
atoms lining the gorge did not move at all, so the docked poses remain valid and
nothing earlier in this work had to be repeated.

  \centering

    force fields, over all 217 built systems.}

We built one system per ligand and not per trajectory, so that a replicate differs
from its parent only in the velocity seed and tests the sampling and not the
structure. The protein was described by the amber14SB force field, the ligand by
OpenFF Sage 2.3.0 through a SMIRNOFF template generator and the solvent by the TIP3P
water model with sodium chloride at \SI{0.15}{\mole\per\litre}. This is the
combination we used for the MM/GBSA calculation of Section~\ref{sec:rungs}, as a
difference there would have turned a comparison of rungs into a comparison of force
fields. Each ligand was placed in its docked pose in a cubic box of \SI{100.1}{\angstrom} per
side, giving between 96{,}220 and 96{,}298 atoms. Table~\ref{tab:system} reports the
composition, where a range is the minimum and the maximum across systems and a single
value means every system shares it. Two quantities were checked for every system instead of being assumed, the first being
the net charge after counterions, which is at most $2.8\times10^{-13}\,e$ across all
217 systems. The second is the shortest receptor--ligand heavy-atom distance at build
time, which runs from 1.67 to \SI{2.64}{\angstrom}. The lower end of that range is a tight contact, which we state openly, and what
resolves it is the minimisation and equilibration that follow.

Each system was energy-minimised by steepest descent until the
maximum force fell below \SI{100}{\kilo\joule\per\mole\per\nano\metre}. It was
then equilibrated for \SI{100}{\pico\second}
in the canonical ensemble with the solute position-restrained and for a further
\SI{100}{\pico\second} in the isothermal-isobaric ensemble without restraints.
Production used a \SI{2}{\femto\second} time step with the bonds to hydrogen
constrained by LINCS, a Verlet neighbour list and particle-mesh Ewald electrostatics.
The cutoff was \SI{1.0}{\nano\metre} for both the real-space Coulomb and the van der
Waals interactions, with a long-range dispersion correction applied to the energy and
the pressure. Temperature was held by the velocity-rescaling thermostat at
\SI{300}{\kelvin} with a coupling time of \SI{0.1}{\pico\second}, and pressure by the
c-rescale barostat at \SI{1}{\bar}, isotropic, with a coupling time of
\SI{2}{\pico\second} and a compressibility of $4.5\times10^{-5}$~\si{\per\bar}, with coordinates written to
disk every \SI{10}{\pico\second}.

We ran the simulations with the GPU-resident mdrun of GROMACS, submitted in batches
through its multi-directory interface with a wall-clock limit per job and restarts
taken from checkpoints. We completed 229 production runs over the 217 ligands, 211 of
them simulated once and 6 three times for the replicate pilot of
Section~\ref{sec:filter}, amounting to 6{,}965~ns. The production length was
\SI{50}{\nano\second} for the first 56 runs and \SI{25}{\nano\second} thereafter, and
17 of those 56 stopped between 34 and \SI{42}{\nano\second} on reaching their
wall-clock limit. The mixture of lengths does not affect the descriptors, since the first
\SI{5}{\nano\second} of every run was discarded and the descriptors were computed on
the \SI{20}{\nano\second} that follows. Every run supplies that window in full, so no
ligand contributes a shorter record than another.

The production ensembles ran at the thermodynamic state they were set to, as shown in
