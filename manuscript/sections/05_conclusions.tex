\section{Conclusions}

In this study we measure what escalating physical detail buys on activity cliffs. The
comparison carries a ligand-based baseline scored on the same frozen splits and a
detection limit fixed before anything had been compared, which is the combination the
existing evidence on this question lacks. It also carries a trajectory for every one of
the 271 ligands, so the dynamic descriptors are scored on the whole set rather than on
the subset an affordable campaign would have reached. The design separates two coefficients of
determination about $0.3$ apart and not less, and that bound was measured in advance
rather than inferred from the outcome.

The two halves of the frozen split do not give the same answer. The development split carries 145 pairs
of human acetylcholinesterase, 77 activity cliffs against 68 matched controls, and
three framings of the endpoint. On it, none of the four structure-based representations
is resolvably better than a two-dimensional fingerprint, and on per-ligand potency the
molecular dynamics descriptors are resolvably worse. On the
sealed test split, opened once after every descriptor set and algorithm had been fixed,
the signed pair difference runs the other way. The receptor ensemble and the dynamic
descriptors lead the fingerprint by $+0.283$ and $+0.311$, and both gaps survive a
correction for the twelve comparisons made and a leave-one-pair-out check. The one
comparison development did resolve, on per-ligand potency, is not reproduced either,
because that gap contains zero on the held-out ligands.

We report that disagreement rather than either half of it. Thirty-eight held-out pairs
against 145 development pairs cannot settle which reading is right, and the candidate
explanations cannot be separated at this size. A held-out sample that happens to suit
low-dimensional descriptors would produce the pattern, and so would the difference
between a cross-validated score and a single refit. What we can say is that both
surviving gaps are about $0.3$, which is exactly the resolution the design was measured
to have, so neither half contradicts that bound. The development split saw nothing of
that size and the held-out split saw something of it.

The instability is itself a result, and it is the one we would carry furthest. A single
frozen split of this size, assembled with connected-component separation and scored
without reselection, can return opposite answers on its two halves on the endpoint that
defines the problem. Benchmarks of activity-cliff prediction are routinely built on one
split of comparable size, and the weight their conclusions can carry is bounded by what
this disagreement shows. That bound applies to the negative result reported here as
much as to any positive one reported elsewhere.

Three further findings transfer beyond this target. First, a replicate test separates
the three design classes sharply. Persistence quantities clear it in 53 of 100
combinations of descriptor and analysis window, fluctuation quantities in 3 of 35, and
the rate of discrete events in 4 of 20. Persistence is therefore what a trajectory of
affordable length can be asked for, and amplitude largely is not. Second, a descriptor
filter refitted at every trajectory length manufactures a rise that disappears once a
single selection is carried across the windows. This exposes any study that re-selects
features at each value of a parameter it is varying. Third, the descriptors that
survive the reproducibility criterion are available from \SI{15}{\nano\second} of
simulation rather than the 50 the original design called for, which answers the cost
objection with a measurement rather than an argument.

What would settle the question this study leaves open is not the length of the
trajectories. The resolution follows from the number of pairs, so more pairs and more
targets are the only things that would narrow it, and they are also what would decide
which half of the split to believe.
